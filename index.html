Welcome: the golden thread

Why this guide exists

This is an illustrated, beginner-friendly companion to the full "MALDI-TOF Study Guide," built around the real Bruker UltrafleXtreme MALDI-TOF/TOF instrument in Aalto University's Molecular Biotechnology lab. It mirrors all 17 topics of that guide, but slows down, adds a figure to every topic, and keeps technical terms paired with a plain-language explanation the first time they appear. It is written for a bachelor's student who has never touched a mass spectrometer before.

Seventeen tabs can feel like a lot of separate facts. They are not. Underneath all of them sit the same five ideas, repeated in a new setting each time. Spotting them as you move through the guide will make every later tab feel like a variation on something you already understand, rather than a new subject.

The five ideas that run through everything

1. One measurement, one equation, every answer. Every single mass this instrument ever reports comes from timing how long an ion takes to fly down a tube, then solving one equation, t = L × sqrt(m / (2zeV)), for mass. Sample prep, calibration, troubleshooting and every case study are all, underneath, about making that one timing measurement as clean and trustworthy as possible.

2. A number is only as good as its calibration. The instrument does not know absolute mass on its own: it has to be shown known masses (calibrants) first, and checked against them regularly. Calibration drift, not a dramatic failure, is the single most common reason a result turns out to be wrong. Every tab on errors, troubleshooting, and judging a "good" result comes back to this.

3. The mode and the matrix are chosen to fit the molecule, not the other way round. Small peptides reward reflector mode's sharper peaks; large, intact proteins need linear mode to survive the flight at all. Different matrices suit different jobs (routine peptides versus sialylated sugars versus intact proteins). Picking the wrong one does not just give a worse answer, it can give no usable answer.

4. One technique, many different questions. The core capability, measuring exact molecular masses very fast, gets reused for strikingly different jobs across this guide: identifying a digested protein from its peptide masses, mapping a sugar chain onto a peptide, reading a whole intact protein end to end without digesting it, and even telling bacterial species apart in a hospital lab. The physics does not change; only the sample, the matrix, the mode, and the question asked of the resulting spectrum change.

5. Confidence is a number, not a feeling. A real identification always comes with a quantitative check: a calibration error in ppm, a MASCOT ions score against a significance threshold, a Biotyper Log(Score) band, a Fragmentation Coverage or Intensity Coverage percentage. Learning to read and judge those numbers, not just the peaks, is what separates a trained user from a beginner.

Overview & How It Works

Purpose

Before you touch any sample or any button on the instrument, you need one clear picture in your head: what is this machine actually doing, and why should you trust the numbers it gives you. This tab builds that picture using the real instrument your course is built around, a Bruker UltrafleXtreme MALDI-TOF/TOF in Aalto University's Molecular Biotechnology lab. Everything later in the guide, sample prep, reading a spectrum, every case study, leans on the ideas introduced here, so it is worth reading slowly even if you plan to skim the rest.

Key ideas

What the name means. MALDI-TOF stands for Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization, Time-Of-Flight mass spectrometry. In plain words, it is a very precise, very fast weighing machine for molecules. You put a biological sample in, and it tells you the exact masses of the molecules inside it, proteins, peptides, or sugar chains, as a list of peaks on a graph.

The real instrument. The Bruker UltrafleXtreme used in this lab can reach a resolution up to 40,000 and an advertised mass accuracy of 1 ppm (one part per million: for a 1000 Da peptide, that is an error of about 0.001 Da). You will work with it through several programs: flexControl (fires the laser and collects spectra), flexAnalysis (processes spectra and picks peaks), ProteinScape (searches a database to identify what you found), GlycoQuest (a glycan search built into ProteinScape), and WARP-LC (automates runs where the sample is separated by liquid chromatography first).

Three things this lab actually uses it for. The one capability, "tell me the masses in this sample," solves several different problems:

  1. Identifying proteins from a digest, by peptide mass fingerprinting (matching peptide masses to a database) plus MS/MS (fragmenting peptides for confirmation).
  2. Identifying glycopeptides, by working out the sugar chain (glycan) and the peptide part together from how the glycopeptide breaks apart.
  3. Sequencing an intact protein end to end without digesting it first, using a technique called in-source decay (ISD), which reads a ladder of fragments straight off the whole protein.

The six-step journey from sample to spectrum. Every MALDI-TOF run follows the same physical sequence, whatever the sample:

  1. Matrix co-crystallization: the sample is mixed with a large excess of a small, light-absorbing chemical, the matrix (commonly HCCA), and dried onto a metal plate so the matrix crystallizes around the sample molecules.
  2. Laser pulse: a short pulse from a nitrogen laser (337 nm, invisible UV light) hits the crystal.
  3. Desorption and ionization: the laser energy vaporizes a tiny volume of the crystal and transfers a proton to some sample molecules, giving them a positive charge, usually just one, written [M+H]+.
  4. Acceleration: a strong electric field (typically 15 to 25 kV) pushes all the newly formed ions down the flight tube, giving every ion approximately the same kinetic energy.
  5. The flight tube (time-of-flight): past the accelerator there is no more field, so ions simply coast through a vacuum. Since they all got the same kinetic energy, lighter ions move faster and arrive sooner.
  6. Detector: a detector at the far end records the exact arrival time of every ion. The software turns each arrival time into a mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) and plots it against how many ions arrived at that time (intensity). That plot is the spectrum.

One extra practical choice sits inside step 5: the instrument can run in linear mode (ions fly straight to a detector at the tube's end) or reflector mode (an electric mirror bounces ions back to a detector near the source, roughly doubling the flight path). Reflector mode gives sharper, more accurate peaks but only works well for smaller molecules; linear mode is used for larger molecules like intact proteins.

The one equation behind all of it. Everything the instrument reports comes from a single measurement, how long an ion takes to cross the flight tube:

t = L × sqrt(m / (2 z e V))

Here t is flight time, L is the length of the flight tube, m is the ion's mass, z is the number of charges on it, e is the elementary charge, and V is the accelerating voltage. The instrument already knows L, e, and V, so measuring t lets it solve for m/z directly. The plain-language version: lighter ions and more highly charged ions arrive sooner; heavier, less-charged ions arrive later. This is also why the axis on every spectrum is labeled "m/z" and not just "mass": almost all MALDI ions carry only one charge (z = 1), so m/z and mass are numerically the same, but the label leaves room for the rare doubly- or triply-charged ion.

Where the tool is strong, and where it struggles. No single instrument is best at everything.

Worked example

Protein A, from the calibration standard used in this lab, shows how the m/z axis and charge state fit together in a real spectrum.

  1. Protein A has a true mass of about 44,613 Da.
  2. Most of the time, MALDI gives it one proton, so it shows up as a singly-charged [M+H]+ ion at m/z 44,613.
  3. Occasionally, MALDI gives the same molecule two protons instead of one. It is now the same molecule, carrying two charges, written [M+2H]2+.
  4. Because m/z is mass divided by charge, that doubly-charged version appears at roughly m/z 22,307, almost exactly half of 44,613.
  5. The lesson: a single real molecule can create two different peaks on the same spectrum, far apart on the x-axis, simply because it picked up a different number of protons during ionization. Reading m/z correctly means always asking "which charge state is this."

Common misconceptions

  • "The instrument puts the sample on a scale and weighs it directly." In reality, it never weighs anything. It measures how long each ion takes to fly down a tube, then calculates mass from that flight time using the equation above.
  • "The number on the x-axis (m/z) is always the molecule's exact mass." Only when the ion carries a single charge (z = 1), which is the most common case in MALDI. A doubly-charged ion of the same molecule appears at roughly half that m/z value, as the Protein A example shows.
  • "MALDI-TOF can tell you exactly how a molecule is built, atom by atom, on its own." Mostly it gives you mass only. Working out structural or linkage detail (such as where a sugar chain attaches) needs extra fragmentation steps or complementary software, covered in later tabs.
  • "Linear mode and reflector mode are basically the same, just pick either one." They trade off sharply: reflector mode gives much sharper peaks but only works well for smaller molecules, while linear mode is needed for very large, intact molecules that would lose signal in the mirror.

What to take forward

Every number this instrument reports traces back to one measured quantity, flight time, and one equation linking flight time to mass and charge; once that clicks, every later tab (sample prep, spectra, troubleshooting) is really just details layered on top of this one idea.

Glossary terms introduced

  • MALDI-TOF: a technique that uses a laser and a light-absorbing matrix to turn a biological sample into flying ions, then measures their masses by timing their flight.
  • Matrix: a small, light-absorbing chemical mixed with the sample that protects it from the laser's energy and helps it become charged.
  • Desorption/ionization: the moment the laser's energy vaporizes part of the sample-matrix crystal and gives some molecules an electrical charge.
  • Time-of-flight (TOF): the part of the instrument where ions travel through a vacuum tube and get sorted by how fast they move.
  • m/z (mass-to-charge ratio): the value plotted on a spectrum's x-axis; equals mass divided by the number of charges on the ion.
  • Reflector mode: a setting where an electric mirror bounces ions back to a detector, giving sharper peaks for smaller molecules.
  • Linear mode: a setting where ions fly straight to a detector without bouncing, used for very large molecules.
  • Resolution: how finely the instrument can tell apart two very close masses.
  • Mass accuracy: how close the instrument's reported mass is to the true mass, often given in parts per million (ppm).
  • PMF (peptide mass fingerprinting): identifying a protein by matching the masses of its digested peptides against a database.
  • ISD (in-source decay): a technique that reads fragments directly off an intact protein without digesting it first.

Mechanism Explained Simply

Purpose

The previous tab explained the six-step MALDI-TOF process with equations and precise terms. This tab has one job: make that same process stick in your memory using everyday comparisons, so that when you walk up to the real instrument, the physics feels familiar rather than abstract. It draws on Fundamentals of MALDI-ToF-MS Analysis (Hosseini and Martinez-Chapa, Springer, 2017).

Key ideas

The matrix as an airbag. Before 1985, mass spectrometry could only ionize samples by blasting them with an electron beam, a "hard" ionization that shattered fragile molecules like proteins into thousands of pieces. In 1985, two German scientists, Karas and Hillenkamp, found a gentler way: mix the fragile molecule with a small, sturdy helper molecule (the matrix) and fire a laser at the mixture. In 2002, Koichi Tanaka won a share of the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for extending this idea to large biological molecules. Think of the matrix as an airbag: the laser energy has to go somewhere, and the matrix absorbs almost all of it instead of your protein. The matrix heats up, flips from solid to gas in an instant, and drags your sample along with it into a small cloud of ions, mostly still in one piece. That protective role is why it is called soft ionization.

The time-of-flight race. Once the cloud of ions exists, an electric field gives every ion the exact same push of energy, like a starting gun firing for every runner in a race at once. With the same push (kinetic energy), a light ion accelerates to a higher speed than a heavy ion, the same way a tennis ball flies faster than a bowling ball if you push both with equal force. So lighter ions cross the flight tube faster and arrive first; heavier ions lag behind. The instrument only ever measures one thing directly, arrival time, then calculates the mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) using m/z = 2eE(t/d)^2, where E is the accelerating voltage, t is flight time, d is the tube length, and e is a physical constant. This is the same equation as tab 1's t = L × sqrt(m / 2zeV), just rearranged and dressed in a foot-race analogy instead of algebra.

Linear mode vs reflectron mode, as a running track. In theory, every ion of the same mass should arrive at exactly the same instant. In practice the laser does not hand out energy perfectly evenly, so ions of the same mass leave with slightly different speeds. In linear mode, that small spread in speed becomes a spread in arrival time, so the peak you see is broad and mushy, like trying to read a car's exact speed from a blurry photograph. Reflectron (mirror) mode fixes this: an electric mirror at the end of the tube reflects the ions back toward a second detector. Ions moving slightly faster penetrate slightly deeper into the mirror's field before turning around, which costs them a little extra time, so slower and faster ions of the same mass arrive back together. It works like sending runners of the same speed around a longer track with a U-turn: the ones who sprinted out too fast spend more time looping back, and the group re-syncs at the finish line.

Linear mode Reflectron (mirror) mode
How it works Ions fly straight to one detector Ions bounce off an electric mirror to a second detector
Resolution Lower; peaks can blur together Much higher; peaks are sharp and well separated
Best for Very large, intact molecules that would fragment or lose signal in the mirror Smaller molecules and peptides where exact mass matters
Trade-off Simpler, more robust signal for huge molecules Adds flight distance and time; huge ions can lose too much signal

Picking a matrix is real chemistry, not a formality. A good matrix must strongly absorb light at the laser's exact wavelength, be mildly acidic so it can donate a proton to help ionize the sample, and crystallize evenly with the sample (co-crystallization) rather than separating out. Seven matrices actually get used in this lab:

Matrix What it's good for
HCCA Peptides and proteins, roughly 0.7 to 20 kDa; default choice for most peptide and protein digest work
2,5-DHB A wide variety of peptides, proteins, polymers and carbohydrates, including phosphopeptides and glycoproteins
2,5-DHAP Proteins from about 8 to 100 kDa; prevents in-source decay fragmentation; good for glycoproteins
SA (sinapinic acid) Larger proteins, roughly 10 to 150 kDa; weak below about 3 kDa
SDHB Very large proteins and glycoproteins; suitable for ISD spectra of intact proteins
3-HPA Oligonucleotides (DNA/RNA), roughly 1 to 30 kDa
1,5-DAN Helps top-down ISD sequencing of intact proteins; hazardous, make fresh immediately before use

Matrices are not perfect: some matrix/sample combinations are only stable for a few minutes before the signal degrades, some matrices self-polymerize and muddy the spectrum, certain matrices worsen mass discrimination, liquid matrices can contaminate the ion source over repeated runs, and matrices with low "protein affinity" cause more unwanted peptide fragmentation. The target plate matters too: ground steel (flat, the default, works for virtually any sample), AnchorChip (800 micron anchor, concentrates a dilute sample for high-throughput runs), BigAnchor (2000 micron, for matrices like DHAP that crystallize poorly on narrower spots), SmallAnchor (400 micron, for oligonucleotides with 3-HPA), and polished steel (flat, for low surface tension samples, must be washed right after use). Across almost all of these, the actual method used in this lab is the simplest one, dried droplet: mix matrix and sample in solution and let the drop dry on the plate.

Mass discrimination: small ions crowd the door. Even with a perfect matrix and perfect prep, MALDI-TOF under-reports heavy ions compared to light ones. Picture the detector as a single narrow doorway during a fire drill: the smallest, fastest people (light ions) reach the doorway first and pack it solid for a moment, so some of the taller, slower people (heavy ions) arriving a fraction of a second later never quite register as clearly. The main fix is turning up the laser power, which pushes more energy into heavier ions, but too much power causes unwanted fragmentation and can push ions to clump together (dimerize). There is no setting that eliminates mass discrimination entirely, only ways to reduce it.

Coupling MALDI with other techniques. MALDI-TOF is often paired with a second technique: ion-mobility TOF (IMS-TOF-MS) separates ions by shape as well as mass, useful for telling apart phosphorylated from non-phosphorylated peptides; FT-ICR-MS gives extremely precise mass measurements using a magnetic field instead of a flight tube; thin-layer chromatography (TLC) and size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) are offline separation steps performed before the sample ever reaches the MALDI plate.

Worked example

This tab states a direct, real decision rule this lab actually follows for choosing mode and matrix. Walking through it step by step:

  1. Ask what you are analyzing. For ordinary peptide work, such as the peptide identification case study elsewhere in this guide, peptides are small and robust.
  2. Because they are small, reflector mode's sharper peaks matter and the peptides survive the extra flight path without losing signal. So: reflector mode + HCCA matrix is the default combination for peptide work.
  3. Now ask about a different case: sequencing an intact, large, fragile protein end to end by in-source decay (ISD), without digesting it first.
  4. Because these molecules are big and fragile, they would lose too much signal, or fragment further, bouncing around in reflector mode.
  5. So the rule flips: linear mode + sinapinic acid (SA) or DHAP is used instead for ISD work on intact proteins.
  6. The underlying logic in both cases is the same one from the table above: match the matrix's mass range and the mode's "best for" column to what you are actually measuring, rather than defaulting to one combination every time.

Common misconceptions

  • "The matrix is just filler, the important chemistry happens to the sample alone." The matrix does most of the work: it absorbs the laser energy, protects the sample from being destroyed, and donates the proton that ionizes the sample.
  • "Reflector mode is always better because it is sharper." It only works well for smaller molecules; very large, intact proteins lose too much signal or fragment further bouncing off the mirror, so linear mode is used for those instead.
  • "If a peak looks small, there must be very little of that molecule in the sample." It might just be a heavy ion suffering from mass discrimination, the built-in bias where heavier ions are under-reported compared to light ones, even if equal amounts of each are present.
  • "Any matrix works for any sample, you just pick your favorite." Matrix choice depends on mass range, chemistry, and even which target plate you use; the wrong matrix can mean poor crystallization, extra fragmentation, or a muddied spectrum.

What to take forward

The matrix protects your sample so it can become a gentle cloud of ions, and the choice of matrix, mode, and target plate is not a formality: it is a real decision, matched to the size and fragility of what you are measuring, that you will make every time you load a plate.

Glossary terms introduced

  • Hard ionization: an older, rougher ionization method (such as electron beam bombardment) that destroys fragile molecules.
  • Co-crystallization: the process of the matrix and sample crystallizing together evenly as a spot dries.
  • Reflectron mode: another name for reflector mode, where an electric mirror re-focuses ions of the same mass so they arrive together, sharpening the peak.
  • Mass discrimination: the instrument's built-in tendency to detect light ions more easily than heavy ones, even when equal amounts are present.
  • Dimerize: when two ions clump together under too much laser power, distorting the apparent mass distribution.
  • Dried droplet method: the common sample prep method of mixing matrix and sample in solution and letting the drop dry on the plate.
  • AnchorChip / BigAnchor / SmallAnchor: target plates with small chemically treated anchor spots of different sizes that concentrate a sample for sharper results.
  • Ion-mobility TOF (IMS-TOF-MS): a coupled technique that separates ions by shape as well as mass.
  • FT-ICR-MS: a technique that measures mass using a magnetic field instead of a flight tube, for extremely high precision.

Sample Prep & Instrument Setup

Purpose

This is where the guide turns from theory into bench work. Good spectra start before the instrument ever fires a laser: how you prepare the sample decides whether you get a clean, identifiable spectrum or noise. This tab walks through the real plate, the real recipe, the real calibration standards, and the real software settings used with the Bruker UltrafleXtreme MALDI-TOF/TOF in Aalto University's Molecular Biotechnology lab.

Key ideas

The target plate. The plate used in this lab holds 384 spots, arranged as 16 rows (A to P) by 24 columns (1 to 24). Ground steel is the default plate for almost everything: peptides, protein digests, and most other biomolecules in acetonitrile or water. Because it is easy to lose track across 384 positions, mark which spots you used on the paper target plate map that comes with the plate. Loading the plate follows a fixed routine: check the access light is green and no plate is loaded, open the inlet door, check the rubber sealing is clean, insert the plate with its frame cut corners first (sample spots facing the machine's longer side), close the door and press Load/Eject, then wait for the status light to turn green before doing anything else. After measuring, press Load/Eject again and wait for green a second time before opening the door.

Preparing a simple peptide sample with HCCA. The most common case in this lab is a plain peptide sample (for example a tryptic digest) spotted with HCCA matrix:

  1. Make sure the sample contains no salts, buffers, or detergents, these will ruin the spectrum; desalt first if needed, for example with a C18 ZipTip.
  2. Prepare a saturated solution of HCCA in TA30 (the standard solvent: 30 percent acetonitrile and 70 percent 0.1% TFA in water, by volume).
  3. Mix one part of your sample solution with one part of the HCCA solution (a 1:1 mix).
  4. Deposit 0.5 microliters of the mixture onto a clean, unused spot and let it dry at room temperature. This is the dried droplet method.
  5. Always spot a calibrant alongside your sample, mixed with the same matrix, so you have something of known mass to calibrate against.

Store all three standards frozen, never refreeze one once thawed, and always use a fresh spot of standard for the detector check too.

Walking through flexControl. flexControl is the program you use to fire the laser and acquire a spectrum. The camera view on the left shows your sample spot, and you can click to move around within it even while shooting. Laser power is adjusted on the right of the camera view: if the signal is weak and noisy, power is too low, increase it; if the baseline does not return to zero between isotope peaks (poor "baseline separation"), power is too high, decrease it. The Add button adds the current spectrum to your running sum, Undo removes the last one added, and you can only fire the laser when the screen says READY. When done, Save As lets you save either Single (individual shots) or Sum (the summed spectrum).

Methods follow a naming convention worth learning: LIFT means MS/MS mode, L means linear mode, R means reflector mode, P means positive ion mode, N means negative ion mode, and any numbers give the mass range. For example, "RP 700-3500" is reflector mode, positive ions, covering 700 to 3500 Da, a typical peptide method.

A few flexControl tabs matter most day to day:

  • Sample Carrier: controls how the plate moves while shooting. Random Walk (moving the plate slightly after a set number of shots) is best left on: use "Complete sample" on normal plates and "Partial sample" on AnchorChip plates.
  • Detection: sets the mass range and sample rate. A sample rate of 2.00 works well for peptides; for large proteins use a lower rate like 1.00, or the file size balloons.
  • Processing: a quick look while shooting; the real processing happens afterwards in flexAnalysis. Centroid picks the whole-peak average mass (better for large molecules whose isotopes cannot be told apart); Snap picks the monoisotopic mass (better for peptides, where isotopes are resolved). A signal-to-noise threshold around 10 is typical for picking peaks at all.
  • Calibration: calibrate against the standard spotted alongside your sample, in the same matrix. Near-neighbour calibration (using the physically closest calibrant spot) gives the best accuracy; on a normal plate, one calibrant spot in the middle of eight sample spots works well. Three calibration modes exist: cubic enhanced (most accurate, needs at least 6 known calibrant masses), quadratic polynomial (needs at least 4), and linear (least accurate, needs only 2, so it suits a narrow mass range). An acceptable calibration error is under 5 ppm for peptides and under 100 ppm for intact proteins.

Keeping the instrument healthy. The detector's sensitivity drifts slowly over time, so run a detector check monthly, and always before an important project, using a fresh spot of HCCA matrix. If the recommended voltage differs from the one currently set by more than 30 V, adjust the detector gain to match.

Lab discipline and why it matters. The MALDI room also houses nano-LC and ESI equipment that is extremely sensitive to contamination, especially from detergents and synthetic polymers like PEG. MS-grade glassware stays in the room, used only with MS-grade solvents, never washed with detergent or put in a dishwasher. Bring your own pipettes for MALDI work rather than using the room's LC/MS-only pipettes, and never pipette directly from a large solvent bottle, use the smaller working bottles instead. Reading a Spectrum

Purpose

Once a sample has been spotted and measured, the instrument hands you a spectrum: a graph of masses and how strongly each one was detected. Everything you will ever conclude about a sample, from "which protein is this" to "did the run work at all," comes from reading that graph correctly. This tab teaches you the real features you will see on every spectrum from this instrument, using the lab's own calibration-standard numbers, so you can tell a trustworthy peak from a misleading one.

Key ideas

The two axes

Every MALDI-TOF spectrum has the same two axes, and reading them correctly is most of what you need to know to use the instrument.

  • X-axis: m/z (mass-to-charge ratio), measured in Daltons (Da). For almost every MALDI ion, this is simply the mass, because almost every ion produced by this method carries a single positive charge. Further right on the axis means a heavier molecule.
  • Y-axis: intensity, in arbitrary units called counts. A taller peak means more ions of that mass arrived at the detector. Importantly, peak height tells you how abundant and how easily-ionized a molecule is, not how biologically important it is. A very abundant, easily-ionized protein can completely overshadow a rarer one that matters much more to your question.

Baseline, noise, and resolution

The wavy or grainy line running along the bottom of a spectrum, between the real peaks, is chemical and electronic noise, not signal. Software removes this automatically (called baseline correction) before it tries to find peaks, but a very noisy baseline is itself a warning sign of a poor-quality spot (tab 5 covers this in detail).

A peak's sharpness tells you about resolution: the instrument's ability to tell two close masses apart. A sharp, narrow peak means close masses were well separated; a broad, fat peak means they were not, and two truly different peaks might be blurred into one bump. Reflector mode gives noticeably better resolution than linear mode, at the cost of being less sensitive to very large molecules, which is why the choice between the two modes is a real decision every time you set up a method.

Isotope patterns

Every peak is technically a small cluster of isotope peaks, spaced roughly 1 Da apart because of naturally occurring heavier isotopes such as carbon-13. At the resolution typically used for large proteins in linear mode, these isotopes blur into one broad peak. Only for smaller, well-resolved molecules, roughly under 3,000 Da and measured in reflector mode, will you actually see the individual isotope "teeth."

Charge state

A peak's position on the x-axis tells you m/z, not mass. If an ion happens to carry two charges instead of one, it shows up at roughly half its true mass, because the charge number divides the mass. A real example from this lab's own calibration standards: Protein A (from Protein Standard II) appears at m/z 44,613 as a singly-charged [M+H]+ ion, and again at roughly m/z 22,307 as a doubly-charged [M+2H]2+ ion, almost exactly half of the first value. Spotting a pair of peaks like this, where one sits at roughly half the m/z of the other, is a useful sanity check that you are reading the spectrum correctly.

Calibrating a spectrum before you trust it

Before any mass on a spectrum can be trusted, the spectrum has to be calibrated against peaks of known mass. In this lab that usually means a Peptide Calibration Standard II spectrum (eight peptides, 700 to 3,500 Da) for peptide work, or a Protein Calibration Standard I spectrum (four proteins, 4,000 to 20,000 Da) for intact proteins, measured on a calibrant spot right next to the sample.

The calibration sequence in flexAnalysis, the software used to process spectra from this instrument, is always the same four clicks: open the calibrant spectrum, then Calibrate, Internal, Automatic assign, Calibrate. Once each real peak has been matched to its known mass and the errors look acceptable, click Calibrate, Copy calibration, select the sample spectra, then Calibrate, Replace calibration to apply that same calibration to them.

You also have to pick how peaks are found in the first place. flexAnalysis offers two peak-picking algorithms: Centroid, which reports a peak's whole average mass and is used for larger molecules whose individual isotopes cannot be separated, and Snap, which reports the monoisotopic mass (the lightest isotope peak in the cluster) and is used once isotopes are resolved, as they typically are for peptides. Picking the wrong one for your mass range quietly shifts every mass you report.

Worked example

Resolution is not an abstract number: it changes what you can actually see in the exact same sample. Take Cytochrome C, average mass 12,360.97 Da, one of the four proteins in Protein Calibration Standard I.

  1. Run it in linear mode. The resolution is around 1,500. On screen this looks like one broad hump: the individual isotope peaks exist in theory, but they blur into a single unresolved envelope.
  2. Run the exact same protein, same sample, in reflector mode. The resolution jumps to around 30,000. Now the isotopes separate into individual, countable teeth.
  3. Nothing about the sample changed between these two runs, only the instrument mode. That is the whole lesson: resolution is something the method gives you, not something fixed by the molecule alone.

This is also why choosing linear versus reflector mode is a real, consequential decision every time a method is set up, not just a textbook distinction.

Common Errors & Troubleshooting

Purpose

Sooner or later a spectrum will look wrong. The good news for a beginner is that on this instrument, a bad result is almost never caused by broken physics. It is almost always calibration drift or contamination, and each of those leaves its own recognizable fingerprint in the spectrum. Learning to recognize those fingerprints, instead of guessing, is what this tab is for.

Key ideas

Crystal and sample problems

Before you even shoot a spot, look at how it crystallized. Good crystals are small and even across the spot. How fast a spot dries, which depends on how much organic solvent is in the matrix solution, controls this: too little organic solvent and the spot dries slowly, producing large, uneven crystals; too much organic solvent and it dries too fast, so the sample never crystallizes properly at all, a visible pattern called the half-moon effect. The usual fix is adjusting the solvent ratio. For a matrix that crystallizes unevenly, such as 2,5-DHAP, it can also help to switch off Random Walk (the setting that moves the laser around the spot automatically) and shoot only inside the ring of crystals that forms at the edge of the dried drop, rather than letting the measurement wander into the empty middle.

Reading the laser power signs

The laser power setting in flexControl, the software that controls the instrument during measurement, tells its own story about what is wrong:

  • High noise together with a weak, low signal means the laser power is too low: increase it.
  • A poor baseline, meaning the signal does not return to the baseline between isotope peaks, means the laser power is too high: decrease it.

Calibration drift

If any single calibrant peak comes back with an error over 10 ppm (parts per million) after calibrating, do not trust that calibration: repeat it. If a detector check shows the recommended voltage has drifted more than 30 V from the current setting, the detector's gain needs adjusting before you can trust anything measured since the last check.

Contamination

Salts, buffers, detergents, and polymers such as PEG all suppress or ruin a MALDI signal. As concrete numbers to keep in mind: PEG is tolerated only up to 0.1% w/v, and SDS, CHAPS, and CHAPSO are flatly incompatible with MALDI at any working concentration. If any of these might be present, desalting the sample first, for example with a C18 ZipTip, is not optional before trying again.

The trypsin autolysis gotcha

If a sample was digested with trypsin, the enzyme itself shows up in the spectrum, not just the protein of interest. Trypsin autolysis peaks regularly appear at m/z 842, 1,045, and 2,678, and they are very easy to mistake for real peptides from the sample. Learn to recognize these three masses on sight and exclude them before sending a peak list to a database search.

A "no hits" checklist

When a MASCOT search through ProteinScape comes back empty, work through these steps in order before concluding the protein simply is not in the database:

  1. Widen the mass tolerance in steps: from 0.1 Da, to 0.5 Da, to 1.0 Da.
  2. Double check the calibration the peak list was measured against.
  3. Check that the taxonomy and database searched were the right ones.
  4. Check that the protease really was trypsin, and not something else.
  5. Check the sample quality itself, going back to the crystal and contamination checks above.

How to tell if a result is good

Every step in this workflow produces some kind of number, and each one has a rough line between "trust it" and "look again":

Step Good value Bad value, and what it usually means
Calibration error Under about 5 ppm for a peptide calibrant Over 10 ppm: the calibration itself is unreliable; repeat it
Detector check Small drift from the last setting Over 30 V drift from the current setting: the detector's gain has degraded and needs adjusting
PMF search tolerance A hit found at the strict default, 0.1 Da Needing to widen to 0.5 or 1.0 Da to get any hit at all: a workable but weaker, less certain result
MASCOT score At or above the significance threshold MASCOT reports (one real example from this lab's own protocol used an ions score threshold of 15.0) Below that threshold: not a statistically significant match, even if it is the top name in the list
Resolution, reflector mode Tens of thousands (about 30,000 for Cytochrome C) A broad, unresolved hump where sharp isotope teeth were expected

A good value is one that is tight, strong, or clearly ahead of its alternatives. A bad one is loose, weak, or ambiguous, which does not automatically mean the result is wrong, only that it needs a second look, a repeat measurement, or a second piece of supporting evidence before you act on it.

Worked example

Suppose a MASCOT search through ProteinScape comes back with no hits at all for a tryptic digest. Here is how you would work through it using the real checklist above, in order:

  1. Widen the tolerance. Start by widening the PMF search tolerance in steps: try 0.1 Da first (the strict default), then 0.5 Da, then 1.0 Da if needed. If a hit now appears at 0.5 Da, that is a workable result, but it is weaker and less certain than a hit found at the strict 0.1 Da default.
  2. Check the calibration. Go back to the calibration run and check each calibrant peak's error. If any single peak is over 10 ppm, the calibration itself cannot be trusted, which would explain why tight tolerances found nothing: repeat the calibration before trying the search again.
  3. Check the taxonomy and database. Confirm the search was pointed at the right organism and the right protein database; a search of the wrong species will not find a match no matter how good the spectrum is.
  4. Check the protease. Confirm the digest really used trypsin, since the search's cleavage rules assume it; a different protease would cut the protein into different pieces than MASCOT is expecting.
  5. Check the sample itself. If all of the above check out, go back to the crystal and contamination checks: uneven crystals or contamination (salts, detergents, PEG above 0.1% w/v) can quietly ruin a signal even when the software settings are all correct.

Working through these five checks in order, rather than jumping straight to "the protein is not in the database," catches the great majority of empty searches.

Case Study: Protein Identification by PMF and MS/MS

Purpose

This tab brings everything together into one real, complete task: starting from a digested protein on the target plate and ending with a confirmed identification. It is worked through using the actual automated and manual workflows this lab's Bruker UltrafleXtreme instrument runs, so you can see how calibration, measurement, and database searching combine into a real result, not just separate skills.

Key ideas

The real identification pipeline

Identifying a protein from a digest on this instrument uses two techniques in sequence: peptide mass fingerprinting (PMF), which matches a list of measured peptide masses against a protein database, followed by MS/MS confirmation, which measures the actual fragment sequence of a few chosen peptides to confirm the match. Both searches run through the MASCOT database, usually via a program called ProteinScape.

The real pipeline runs through four pieces of software, with flexControl and flexAnalysis each used twice:

  1. flexControl measures MS spectra of the sample spots and the calibrant spots side by side.
  2. flexAnalysis detects the peaks and calibrates the sample spectra using near-neighbour calibration (borrowing the calibration fit from the nearest calibrant spot).
  3. The resulting peak list goes back to flexControl, which measures MS/MS spectra, using the LIFT cell, on a chosen set of peaks.
  4. flexAnalysis detects the MS/MS peaks.
  5. Both the MS and MS/MS peak lists are sent to ProteinScape, which hands them to the MASCOT database for the actual search.

Running it automatically: ProteinScape and AutoXecute

The automated version of this pipeline starts in ProteinScape, not at the instrument itself. In condensed form:

  1. In ProteinScape, create a new project and a new sample to hold your digest (a sample can hold one or several spectra).
  2. Load the target plate in flexControl and shoot one spot manually first to check the method and laser power look right.
  3. Shoot the calibration spot into the sum buffer and calibrate before going further.
  4. In flexControl's AutoXecute tab, start the run wizard: pick the plate type, choose which spots to measure, and link the run to the matching ProteinScape sample. The number of plate spots and ProteinScape samples must match exactly, or automatic processing fails.
  5. Pick or edit the AutoXecute and flexAnalysis methods for calibration, MS, and MS/MS (these methods carry the real shot counts and tolerances below).
  6. In ProteinScape, configure Automatic MS searches, then Automatic MS/MS searches, with the chosen search parameters.
  7. Click Start automatic run in flexControl. The instrument now runs the whole pipeline on its own.
  8. Results land back in ProteinScape, with MASCOT hits appearing under the Proteins and Peptides tab, ready to inspect.

The automated run has real, concrete shot counts behind it:

  • Calibration: roughly 1,500 satisfactory shots.
  • MS measurement: roughly 2,000 satisfactory shots.
  • MS/MS parent mode: 800 satisfactory shots, collected in steps of 50 at a time.
  • MS/MS fragment mode: 4,000 satisfactory shots, collected in steps of 100 at a time.

Fragment mode needs far more shots than parent mode because the fragment signal is much weaker than the parent peak it came from, so more shots are needed to build a clean, readable fragment spectrum.

Setting the tolerances

The PMF search's default mass tolerance is a strict 0.1 Da, which only works if the calibration was excellent. If a search comes back with no hits, the usual fix is to widen the tolerance in steps, first to 0.5 Da, then if needed 1.0 Da (tab 5 covers the full troubleshooting checklist for this).

Once peak lists reach MASCOT itself, tolerances are expressed differently: the MS tolerance is commonly set around 40 ppm, while the MS/MS tolerance is looser, up to 1,000 ppm, since fragment peaks are weaker and less precisely measured than the parent peaks they came from.

Two modifications are used by default on every search here:

  • Global modification Carbamidomethyl (C): accounts for the alkylation step in sample preparation, which permanently and uniformly modifies every cysteine, so it is set as a fixed rule rather than something the search has to guess at.
  • Variable modification Oxidation (M): methionine oxidizes spontaneously during sample handling and storage, so the search allows for it, but only as an optional possibility, since not every methionine will actually be oxidized.

The manual route: BioTools and MASCOT

This lab also runs a manual alternative using BioTools instead of ProteinScape: measure and calibrate the MS spectrum as usual, send the peak list to BioTools, then open its MASCOT search window and set the real parameters (missed cleavages Partial, typically allowing 1; database NCBI or Swissprot; MS tolerance around 40 ppm; the same two modifications as above). Peaks chosen for confirmation are sent back to flexControl as an MS/MS list, where a LIFT method is opened and the PCIS window (the mass window around the parent ion) is widened if needed, typically to roughly 0.43% of the parent mass. Parent mode is shot with PLMS (Post-LIFT Metastable Suppression) switched off, then fragment mode is shot and summed until the fragment ladder is clean.

Worked example

Start with a protein digest spotted with HCCA matrix on a ground steel target, with a Peptide Calibration Standard II spot alongside it. flexControl measures the MS spectrum of both spots, and flexAnalysis finds the peaks and calibrates the sample spectrum against the calibrant spot.

  1. Pick candidate peaks. The resulting peak list usually has dozens of peaks, but only a handful, typically 4 to 8 of the highest-intensity, well-separated peaks, get chosen for MS/MS confirmation.
  2. Exclude the trypsin peaks. One strong peak sits right at m/z 842. This is a trypsin autolysis peak, a leftover fragment of the digestion enzyme itself, not a fragment of the sample protein, so it is excluded from the MS/MS list. The same applies to peaks at 1,045 and 2,678 Da.
  3. Measure MS/MS on the rest. The remaining strong peaks are sent to flexControl as an MS/MS list. flexControl switches to a LIFT method and measures each one in turn: parent mode in 50-shot steps up to 800 total shots, then fragment mode in 100-shot steps up to 4,000 total shots, building a readable ladder of b-type and y-type fragment ions.
  4. Search. Both the MS peak list and the MS/MS peak lists go to ProteinScape, which searches MASCOT using the tolerances above. A confident result comes back as a high-scoring protein hit, with several tryptic peptides matched and the MS/MS spectra confirming their exact sequences.

Fine-tuning a low-scoring hit with simple arithmetic

Sometimes MASCOT returns a correct but low-scoring hit because automatic peak-picking missed a couple of fragment ions in the y-ion ladder (the series of ions counted from the C-terminal end of the peptide). When that happens, you do not have to accept the lower score: you can often calculate exactly where the missing peaks should be and confirm them by eye.

Suppose the y-ion ladder is missing two expected ions, y12 and y11, but y13 is clearly present at m/z 1,412. Each step down the ladder subtracts the mass of one amino acid, in the order the peptide sequence predicts:

  • The amino acid between y13 and y12 is proline, which weighs 97 Da. So y12 should sit at 1,412 minus 97, which is 1,315.
  • The next amino acid, between y12 and y11, is glutamine, which weighs 128 Da. So y11 should sit at 1,315 minus 128, which is 1,187.

Checking the spectrum at m/z 1,315 and m/z 1,187 finds small peaks that were there all along, just not picked up automatically. Adding and annotating these two peaks by hand (both flexAnalysis and BioTools have an amino-acid annotation tool for exactly this) and re-running the search gives the full sequence recognized, with a noticeably higher score than before.

TOF/TOF, LIFT & Intact Proteins (ISD)

Purpose

So far in this study guide, the instrument has mostly been used to weigh whole molecules. This tab explains two more advanced tricks the same MALDI-TOF/TOF instrument can do: breaking a peptide apart on purpose so you can read its sequence from the pieces, and reading a long stretch of a whole protein's sequence without cutting it into peptides at all. Both tricks rely on the same physics of flight time, so understanding one helps you understand the other.

Key ideas

How breaking a peptide apart actually produces a readable spectrum

In the first flight tube, called TOF1, ions separate out by mass the normal way: lighter ions arrive first, heavier ions arrive later. While this is happening, some peptide ions break apart on their own (called laser-induced decay, or LID), or are made to break apart on purpose by colliding with a gas such as argon (called collision-induced decay, or CID). The key physical fact is this: a fragment and the parent ion it broke off from keep travelling at exactly the same speed as each other, because splitting apart mid-flight changes an ion's mass but not its speed.

A gate called the PCIS (PreCursor Ion Selector) uses that fact to pick out one specific peptide from a whole mixture. It lets through only the chosen parent ion and the fragments still travelling at its speed, and blocks everything else. Anything not travelling at the parent's speed never makes it through the gate.

The ions that pass through the PCIS then enter the LIFT cell (a second ion source) for reacceleration. This step exists because a lighter fragment, moving at the same speed as its heavier parent, carries proportionally less kinetic energy (the energy of motion), not less speed. Left alone, the small fragments would arrive at the detector with too wide a spread of energies to all be captured sharply at one fixed reflector voltage. The LIFT cell gives every surviving ion an extra push of voltage, which brings even the lightest fragment's energy back up close to the same share as the parent's. That way the whole fragment spectrum can be read at once, at a single fixed reflector voltage, instead of scanning through many different voltages.

Reading a whole protein's sequence without digesting it first (ISD)

Normally, to identify a protein you cut it into peptides first and read those (this is called tryptic digestion, and it is covered elsewhere in this guide). But this instrument can also read a long stretch of sequence directly off a whole, undigested protein, through a phenomenon called in-source decay, or ISD. Some of the protein's backbone bonds spontaneously break apart right in the ion source, before the ions even start flying. This produces two separate ladders of fragments in the resulting spectrum: one ladder counting in from the protein's N-terminus (the start of the chain) and one counting in from its C-terminus (the end of the chain). Reading the gap between two neighbouring rungs on either ladder tells you the mass, and so the identity, of the amino acid at that position.

Getting a good ISD spectrum needs a different recipe from a normal peptide measurement:

  • Matrix: sinapinic acid or DHAP (2,5-dihydroxyacetophenone). DHAP crystallizes as a ring around the rim of the dried droplet rather than evenly across the whole spot, so when using DHAP, the usual "random walk" shooting pattern (which spreads laser shots evenly across a spot) is switched off, and shots are aimed only inside that ring.
  • Linear mode, not reflector mode, the same choice made elsewhere in this guide for large, fragile molecules.
  • High laser power, around 70%, and many shots, around 5,000, moving the laser around the spot as you go to build up that many shots.

Reading the ladder out in software (rather than just eyeballing the raw spectrum) uses the instrument software's own ISD tools: the spectrum is permanently marked as ISD-type before MS/MS processing can begin; a "TDS mascot search" (top-down sequencing) is run with no enzyme selected, no carbamidomethylation, a loose 50 ppm mass tolerance, and the instrument set to MALDI-ISD; and a dedicated annotation mode reads the ladder out amino acid by amino acid. One real wrinkle: ISD does not break the protein backbone at proline, so a proline in the sequence shows up as a missing rung rather than a wrong one.

Worked example

The LIFT reacceleration math. A parent ion of 1000 Da, accelerated at 8 kV in the first ion source, ends up with a kinetic energy of 8 keV (call that 100%). A 100 Da fragment of that same parent comes out of the first flight tube with only 10% of that energy, 0.8 keV, because it is moving at the same speed but carries only a tenth of the mass.

After a 19 kV LIFT reacceleration, the parent's energy becomes 27 keV (still counted as 100%), and the small 100 Da fragment's energy becomes 19.8 keV, about 73% of the parent's energy. That is close enough, across the whole range of fragment masses, for one fixed reflector voltage to capture the entire fragment spectrum in one go. Without the LIFT step, that same 100 Da fragment would have stayed at only 10% of the parent's energy, far too different to be captured sharply alongside the parent and the other fragments.

Why this matters for reading real spectra. The peptide FQSEEQQQTEDELQDK loses 98 Da (H3PO4, phosphoric acid) when it is fragmented this way in MS/MS. That kind of loss is a classic sign of phosphorylation, because a phosphate group readily falls off as neutral phosphoric acid during fragmentation. This same peptide and the same phosphorylation show up again elsewhere in this guide, but spotted from the opposite direction: as a +80 Da mass increase on the whole, unfragmented peptide rather than a 98 Da loss after fragmentation. Both are two sides of the same diagnostic coin.

Common misconceptions

  • "Breaking a peptide apart changes how fast the pieces fly." It does not. A fragment and the parent ion it came from travel at the same speed; only their energy and mass differ, because speed only changes when fragmentation happens before the first acceleration, not mid-flight.
  • "The LIFT cell is just there to speed everything up." Its real job is narrower: it corrects an energy mismatch. Without it, light fragments would arrive with too little energy relative to heavy fragments for one fixed reflector voltage to focus them all sharply.
  • "ISD is just a worse version of normal digestion." ISD answers a different question. Normal digestion identifies a protein by matching many small peptide masses to a database. ISD reads a direct sequence ladder off one intact protein, which is useful for checking things digestion cannot easily show, such as whether a specific end of the protein chain still carries an N-terminal or C-terminal modification.
  • "A gap in an ISD ladder always means a sequencing error." Not always: ISD does not cleave the backbone at proline, so a true gap at a proline position is expected and is accounted for by the annotation software, not a sign that something went wrong.

  • TOF1: the first flight tube in a TOF/TOF instrument, where ions first separate out by mass.
  • Laser-induced decay (LID): a peptide ion breaking apart on its own, without being deliberately collided with a gas.
  • Collision-induced decay (CID): a peptide ion being made to break apart by colliding with a gas such as argon.

Case Study: Glycopeptides

Purpose

A plain peptide has one fixed mass: cut it with an enzyme and you get the same number every time. A glycopeptide is that same peptide wearing a sugar chain that can vary enormously in size and shape from one copy to the next, so the glycopeptide's total mass keeps shifting even though the peptide underneath never changes. This tab explains the real software settings a lab uses to pull those two things apart: the fixed peptide, and the variable sugar sitting on top of it.

Key ideas

Why a fixed mass shift works for one modification, but not for a glycopeptide

A standard database search finds a chemical modification by looking for a fixed mass offset. For example, the peptide FQSEEQQQTEDELQDK has an unmodified calculated mass of MH+ 1981.8 Da. Its phosphorylated form comes in at MH+ 2061.8 Da, a clean +80 Da that software can search for directly, because every phosphate group adds the same amount of mass no matter which protein it sits on.

A glycopeptide cannot be searched this way. The same peptide backbone, at the same glycosylation site, can carry ten or more different sugar chains, each adding a different mass to the total, so there is no single number to add. This means glycopeptide software needs a separate strategy: first work out that a spectrum belongs to a glycopeptide at all, then work out the peptide's mass separately from the sugar's mass.

Spotting a glycopeptide spectrum: oxonium ions and mass gaps

A glycopeptide-recognition tool filters MS/MS spectra in two steps. First, it checks for diagnostic oxonium ions, small sugar-only fragments that appear at the same fixed masses no matter what protein they came from, such as 204 Da and 366 Da. Second, it checks for diagnostic mass distances between peaks: 162 Da for a hexose sugar, 203 Da for a HexNAc sugar, and 291 Da for a sialic acid (also called NeuAc). Any spectrum that shows these signatures is flagged as a likely glycopeptide, and the tool then works out a likely mass for the peptide alone by reading backward from those same sugar-specific patterns.

On top of this, there is a more specific signature: the last HexNAc sugar still attached to the peptide tends to break apart in a recognisable three-gap pattern, 17, 83, and 120 Da apart. This pattern comes from cross-ring cleavage of that terminal HexNAc, right at the asparagine glycosylation site. It is a separate, smaller-scale phenomenon from the 162/203/291 Da gaps and the 204/366 Da oxonium ions, so the two sets of numbers should not be treated as interchangeable.

The actual settings used for this recognition step: classification method "N-glycopeptide MALDI", a distance tolerance of 0.5 Da, a peptide mass offset of H+ (1.008 Da), and a minimum peptide mass of 800 Da. A stricter minimum than the usual default of 600 Da is used deliberately, because false matches from random short "peptides" are more likely to appear at low mass.

Matrix and mode: a correctness decision, not just a sensitivity one

Which matrix and which mode to use is not only about getting a strong signal. On a glycopeptide, it changes what the spectrum actually shows. Neutral, non-sialylated glycopeptides are run with HCCA matrix in reflector mode. Acidic, sialylated glycopeptides must be run with 2,5-DHB or 2,5-DHAP matrix in linear mode, not reflector mode, because reflector mode causes sialic acid groups to break off mid-flight through metastable decay. A sialylated glycopeptide run in reflector mode would give a wrong picture of the real sugar pattern; only linear mode shows the true one.

Searching for the glycan itself

Once a spectrum is flagged as a glycopeptide and a peptide mass has been worked out, a second search tool looks for the glycan itself, treating the peptide mass as a known, fixed offset rather than something it needs to solve. Typical settings: glycan type "N-glycan core basic", a public glycan database, underivatized samples, a free reducing end, H+ ions, an MS tolerance of 0.7 Da, and an MS/MS tolerance of 0.9 Da. The match score for this search has two parts: Fragmentation Coverage % (what fraction of the expected glycan fragments were actually seen) and Intensity Coverage % (how much of the total signal those matched fragments account for).

Worked example

EEQFNSTFR is a real tryptic glycopeptide from mouse IgG1, carrying an N-glycosylation site at the asparagine (N). Its MS/MS spectrum shows a spread of peaks across the full mass range, and reading them step by step shows exactly how the recognition logic above plays out on a real spectrum:

  1. Low-mass oxonium ions appear first. Peaks at 204.102 Da and 366.085 Da sit close to the 204 Da and 366 Da oxonium ion signatures, immediately flagging this spectrum as a likely glycopeptide.
  2. A ladder of glycan fragments fills the middle of the spectrum. Peaks such as 138.081, 241.089, 423.194, 735.338, 982.595, 1,709.862, and 2,399.432 Da are all glycan-moiety fragments, pieces of the sugar chain breaking apart at different points.
  3. The bare peptide mass shows up as its own region. Around 1,506.768 Da, a peak labelled "Peptide MH+" in the lab's own training material marks something close to the bare peptide mass, exactly the number the recognition software works out from the surrounding glycan pattern.
  4. The full, intact glycopeptide appears at the high-mass end. A peak at 2,618.544 Da is labelled "[Peptidoglycan] MH+", the complete peptide plus its attached sugar chain together, with the highest-mass peak in the spectrum observed at 2,764.071 Da.

Common misconceptions

  • "A glycopeptide's mass can be searched the same way as a phosphopeptide's." It cannot. A phosphate group always adds a fixed +80 Da, but a glycan's mass varies hugely from one copy of the same peptide to the next, so there is no single fixed offset to search for.

Proteomics and PTMs

Purpose

Earlier tabs in this guide use MALDI-TOF to match a whole, intact protein against a species database. This tab goes one level deeper: cutting proteins into peptides, weighing those peptides precisely, and using the exact numbers to answer a sharper question: has this protein been chemically altered after it was built? Spotting a post-translational modification (PTM), a small chemical change added to a protein after it is made, matters because PTMs switch genes on and off, turn cell signalling on or off, and mark proteins for destruction, in human cells, bacteria, and yeast alike.

Key ideas

Bottom-up proteomics: cutting proteins down to measurable pieces

Studying a protein's modifications usually starts with a "bottom-up" workflow, so called because it builds understanding up from small peptide pieces rather than reading the whole protein at once:

  1. Lyse the cells. Human cells break open with a mild detergent buffer. Bacteria and yeast are tougher: bacteria often need an enzyme (lysozyme) to weaken the cell wall first, and yeast usually needs mechanical force (glass-bead beating), because its wall is thicker still.
  2. Reduce and alkylate. A reducing agent breaks disulfide bonds (sulfur-sulfur links that hold a folded protein's shape together) so the protein unfolds completely, and an alkylating agent then caps the freed sites so they cannot re-form. This step is also why a fixed, known mass tag shows up on every cysteine later in a database search, a tag from this chemistry step, not from any real biology.
  3. Digest with trypsin. Trypsin is an enzyme that reliably cuts a protein chain right after every lysine or arginine, turning one large, hard-to-weigh protein into a predictable set of small peptides that are each easy to measure precisely. Digestion typically runs overnight, well over 10 hours, at around 30 degrees Celsius (not 37, which causes evaporation problems over that long a run).
  4. Clean up (desalt) and spot with matrix, the same matrix step used throughout this guide.
  5. Acquire the spectrum. The result is a peptide mass fingerprint (PMF): a list of exact peptide masses.
  6. Search a database. Software predicts what masses trypsin should have produced from every protein in a reference database, allowing for extra mass additions at specific amino acids (the PTMs), and finds the best match. A peak that lines up with a predicted mass plus a fixed extra amount is how a modification gets identified, not just a protein.

Reading a mass shift: the core trick behind every example in this tab

A PTM is a small, specific chemical group tacked onto one amino acid. Because that group always has the same mass, it always shifts the peptide's peak by the same fixed number of daltons (Da), no matter which protein it sits on. Once you know the shift, spotting the modification is just arithmetic: subtract the expected unmodified mass from the mass you actually measured.

Enriching and confirming phosphorylation

Phosphorylation is common but tricky to see directly, because phosphopeptides are usually rare in a digest and ionize poorly in positive-ion mode (the phosphate group's negative charge works against picking up the proton MALDI needs to detect it). The workflow below exists specifically to work around that:

Enrichment comes first, always: IMAC resin or titanium/zirconium dioxide beads selectively grab phosphopeptides out of a digest, because unmodified peptides would otherwise drown out the weak phosphopeptide signal. Then the search looks for the +80 Da mass shift, since a phosphate group (HPO3) adds almost exactly 80 Da (79.9663 Da, to be precise) per phosphorylated residue. Fragmenting the peptide to find exactly which residue carries the phosphate comes with a catch: phosphoserine and phosphothreonine are chemically fragile under standard fragmentation and tend to lose the whole phosphate group as neutral H3PO4, a 98 Da drop, before the backbone itself breaks apart. That loss is a great confirmation signal but a poor localization tool, since it dominates the spectrum and starves out the very fragments that would reveal the exact residue. Phosphotyrosine does not have this problem; it is more chemically stable and fragments normally.

Worked example

This is the central worked example of this tab, reused from elsewhere in this guide: the peptide FQSEEQQQTEDELQDK, from beta casein (a real milk protein). Its unmodified, calculated mass is MH+ 1981.8 Da. The phosphorylated form, FQpSEEQQQTEDELQDK (the lowercase p marks the phosphate, sitting on the serine), comes in at MH+ 2061.8 Da.

  1. Find the difference. 2061.8 minus 1981.8 equals 80.0 Da.
  2. Match it to the table above. An 80 Da shift is the signature of phosphorylation; nothing else in the common PTM list matches that number.
  3. Check it makes biological sense. Beta casein is a well-known, naturally phosphorylated milk protein, so a clean +80 Da peak here fits what is already known about it.
  4. Predict the confirmation experiment. Treat a split of the same sample with a phosphatase enzyme (which strips phosphate groups) and re-run it on MALDI-TOF: the 2061.8 Da peak should disappear, and the 1981.8 Da peak should reappear.

A live example close to home. Aalto University's own Cellular Engineering research group, led by Assistant Professor Sesilja Aranko (Department of Bioproducts and Biosystems), works on exactly this kind of problem: installing post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation, hydroxylation, and glycosylation, onto recombinant structural proteins such as silk and collagen that are produced in bacteria, which cannot make these modifications on their own. The aim is to understand how a modification changes how the protein folds and assembles, then use that to engineer sustainable, functional biomaterials as alternatives to oil- and animal-based materials. This work is funded in part by a Novo Nordisk Foundation Emerging Investigator Grant (the Pro2Fun project, roughly 1.34 million euro over five years), and a Research Council of Finland project called CrossSilk looks at a related problem: improving the water resistance of silk and cellulose-based biomaterials. Verifying that a phosphate group (or any other PTM) really did attach, and only where intended, is exactly the kind of check a +80 Da mass shift on a MALDI-TOF spectrum is built for.

A closely related, published example (not from Aranko's own group, but in the same research area): a 2025 paper in Trends in Biotechnology reported producing phosphorylated and functional alpha-s1-casein, another real milk casein protein, in E. coli, by co-expressing two bacterial protein kinases alongside the casein gene. Mass spectrometry confirmed the phosphorylation, and the phosphorylated recombinant casein bound calcium and behaved much more like real bovine casein than the unphosphorylated version did.

A second worked example, reading bacterial drug resistance as a mass shift. The meropenem hydrolysis assay incubates live bacteria directly with the antibiotic meropenem and asks whether the drug's own mass changes. Intact meropenem shows up at m/z near 384. If a bacterial enzyme called a carbapenemase is present, it breaks open the drug's ring by adding a water molecule, a +18 Da mass shift, producing a new peak at m/z near 402. Testing two unknown bacterial isolates after a 3-hour incubation: isolate A shows a single strong peak at m/z 384 and nothing else, meaning the drug is unchanged and no carbapenemase is active; isolate B shows a peak at m/z 402 and almost no peak at 384, meaning the drug's ring was hydrolyzed and an active carbapenemase (commonly KPC, NDM, or VIM) is present. This test gives a result in as little as 3 to 4 hours, compared with the 1 to 2 days a traditional culture-based test takes, though a clinical lab would still confirm the specific resistance gene with a separate test afterward.

A harder case: O-GlcNAcylation. This modification adds a single sugar (GlcNAc) onto serine or threonine and behaves biologically like phosphorylation's twin, often competing for the exact same residues. Its mass shift (+203 Da) is the same mass as any single HexNAc sugar, so mass alone cannot tell it apart from other sugars, and like a phosphate group, it tends to pop off as a neutral loss before the backbone breaks, blocking exact site-mapping. Unlike N-glycosylation, it has no strict sequence rule and no simple release enzyme, so confirming it usually needs extra chemical tagging steps rather than a simple mass search.

Scale in real research. A yeast study tracked Saccharomyces cerevisiae across heat-shock and cold-stress conditions and quantified 9,357 phosphosites on 2,344 proteins, finding that 388 sites changed dynamically and about 36% of those responded to both heat and cold stress. A separate human-cell study built calibration curves from seven synthetic peptide pairs mixed in known ratios, and found that the matrix used (CHCA, DHB, or THAP) changes the measured ratio for the same sample, with reproducibility across repeats of only 5 to 10%, a reminder that MALDI-TOF is far more reliable for answering "is this modified" than "how much is modified".

  • "Every PTM can be found by searching for one fixed mass offset." Only PTMs with a truly fixed chemical mass work this way, such as phosphorylation (+80 Da) or acetylation (+42 Da). LC-MALDI and Automated Pipelines

Purpose

Real samples in a lab are almost never one clean, simple molecule. A real protein digest contains dozens or hundreds of different peptides at once, and if you spot that whole mixture straight onto a MALDI plate, something goes wrong before you even get to the measurement. This tab explains that problem and the automated fix built around it, so that when you later see a real LC-MALDI run, you understand what each step is actually solving.

Key ideas

The problem: winners and losers

When a complex peptide mixture is spotted directly onto the MALDI target, the different peptides compete with each other for ionization (turning into charged particles that the instrument can detect) inside the MALDI source. The peptides present in high amounts, the "winners," ionize strongly and dominate the spectrum. The peptides present in low amounts, the "losers," can be pushed down almost completely. This effect is called ion suppression. A second problem shows up too: some peptides sit so close together in mass that MALDI-TOF/TOF cannot cleanly pick out just one of them for fragmentation (MS/MS) without also grabbing its neighbor.

The fix: separate first, spot later

The solution is to pull the mixture apart before it ever touches the MALDI plate. The peptides are pumped through a column packed with a non-polar material, usually C18, in a technique called reversed-phase liquid chromatography, or LC. As a gradient of acetonitrile in water increases over time, each peptide clings to the C18 material for a while before washing off, and the more hydrophobic (water-avoiding) a peptide is, the longer it clings. The result is that peptides leave the column one after another, roughly from least to most hydrophobic, instead of all mixed together.

Instead of feeding this separated LC output straight into a mass spectrometer in real time, the way it is normally done with electrospray ionization (ESI, see tab 14), the eluate (the liquid coming off the column) is spotted onto a MALDI target instead: one small LC fraction per spot, placed there by a dedicated fraction collector called the Bruker PROTEINEER fc II. This single change splits two timescales that used to be stuck together. The LC separation itself only takes minutes, but once a fraction is sitting on the plate, the slow and careful MALDI measurement that follows has no time pressure at all. The whole separation is effectively frozen onto the target, ready to be read calmly, one spot at a time.

WARP-LC: running it all automatically

Doing this by hand, spot by spot, would undo the whole point of automating the workflow, so a piece of software called WARP-LC runs it instead. Once every fraction has been spotted and measured, WARP-LC tracks each peptide (which it calls a "compound") across the several neighboring spots it shows up in, by matching retention time (how long it took to come off the LC column) and mass. For each peptide, WARP-LC then picks the single best spot to send for MS/MS fragmentation: the one with the highest signal-to-noise, so the same peptide is never fragmented twice. It also filters out background compounds automatically and schedules the whole batch of MS/MS runs so none of it is wasted repeating the same measurement.

Why the extra step pays off

Separating first and spotting later brings real, named benefits: less ion suppression, because the mixture is already spread out before it reaches the MALDI source; no acquisition time pressure, because the separation is already frozen onto the plate rather than flowing past in real time; and deeper, more complete identification overall. One more benefit is easy to miss: because the separation is physically preserved on the plate, the same plate can be measured again later, for example to go back and chase a peptide that was missed the first time.

Taken further: comparing samples (advanced context)

Once a lab is already running LC-MALDI, it is a short step to compare several samples against each other in the same run, using isotope labels. iTRAQ labels are isobaric, meaning they add the same total mass to a peptide no matter which sample they came from, and they allow up to 8 samples to be compared side by side in one run (called 8-plex) without adding extra complexity to the plain MS spectrum, though every peptide still needs its own MS/MS scan to read out how much of it was in each sample. SILAC and ICPL labels work the other way around: their quantitation signal is already visible at the plain MS level, but they only scale up to 4 samples at once (4-plex). This is advanced context rather than something the basic LC-MALDI workflow strictly needs, but it shows how far the same pipeline can be pushed.

Worked example

Here is the actual spotting recipe that turns an LC fraction into a readable MALDI spot, worked through step by step.

  1. The LC eluate coming off the column and a matrix solution are mixed together in a small mixing chamber called a MicroTee.
  2. That mixture is deposited onto an 800 micron AnchorChip target (the same anchor chip format used elsewhere in this guide) using HCCA matrix.
  3. For the mixture to crystallize well on the chip, it needs to be around 85% organic solvent.
  4. If there is too little organic solvent, the spot dries slowly and forms crude crystals. If there is too much, it dries too fast and forms an amorphous mess that the laser cannot read well. The 85% figure is the balance point between those two failure modes.
  5. Each finished spot ends up with only about 420 nanolitres of matrix solution on it, a tiny volume that still has to crystallize evenly enough for the laser to find a good signal reliably, spot after spot, run after run.

This is why LC-MALDI needs careful, consistent chemistry at the spotting stage: hundreds of spots have to crystallize the same reliable way for WARP-LC to be able to compare them automatically afterward.

  • "A complex digest can be spotted directly, the same way a simple sample is." In reality, high-abundance peptides suppress low-abundance ones through ion suppression, so a direct spot of a complex mixture loses information that LC separation would have kept.

Other Applications Beyond This Lab

Purpose

This lab's own MALDI-TOF/TOF instrument is set up for one job: identifying proteins and peptides. MALDI as a technology is used for much more than that elsewhere, and the name "MALDI-TOF" is probably most familiar to people from a completely different use, rapid bacterial identification in a hospital lab. Knowing these other uses matters so that when you hear "MALDI-TOF" outside this lab, you can place it correctly instead of assuming it always means what this lab's instrument does.

Key ideas

Clinical bacterial identification

The use most people associate with the words "MALDI-TOF" is rapid clinical bacterial identification, the approach behind Bruker's Biotyper and similar systems. Instead of digesting a protein with an enzyme, a whole bacterial cell is smeared directly onto the plate. Its abundant ribosomal proteins (proteins that are part of the cell's protein-building machinery, present in huge numbers in every cell), in the mass range roughly 2,000 to 20,000 Da, produce a fingerprint that gets matched against a reference spectral library. This approach has a strong real-world track record: during the 2011 German outbreak of E. coli O104:H4, screening clinical isolates for two characteristic marker peaks by MALDI-TOF correctly classified 292 of 293 isolates as outbreak-related or not, about 99.7% accuracy. This lab's instrument is a protein and peptide research tool, not a clinical bacterial-identification system, so this is not a workflow it runs, but it is the application most people have actually heard of.

Reading a real Biotyper-style result

A Biotyper-style identification reports more than just a species name, and it is worth knowing how to read the full result. The raw spectrum is a whole-cell fingerprint, not a digest: its mass axis runs from 0 to 20,000 Da, the typical window where abundant ribosomal and other highly expressed proteins survive a crude smear preparation. A handful of sharp peaks rise out of a noisy, sloping baseline; that baseline is normal for this kind of crude, unpurified sample, not itself a sign of a bad measurement.

Underneath the spectrum, the software ranks library matches by Log(Score), the number that actually matters, not just which species name appears first in the list. The standard scoring convention (general knowledge, not specific to this lab) reads:

Log(Score) What it means
2.300 to 3.000 Highly probable species identification
2.000 to 2.299 Secure genus identification, probable species identification
1.700 to 1.999 Probable genus identification only
Below 1.700 Not a reliable identification

Metabolomics

MALDI-TOF is also used to fingerprint small molecules, called metabolites (such as sugars, lipids and amino acids), rather than proteins; this field is called metabolomics. One real example: a MALDI-MS study of lung cancer cell lines tracked a membrane lipid, measuring it at m/z 804 in its original form and at m/z 802 after the cells were treated with an omega-3 fatty acid called EPA, which the lipid had incorporated. One cell line picked up 51% more of the EPA-containing lipid after treatment, versus only 12% in a second cell line, a real metabolic difference measured directly by mass rather than guessed at indirectly. Metabolomics uses a different extraction method, a different matrix, and a different mass range from the peptide and protein work this lab's instrument runs.

Polymers, biosensors, tissue engineering and drug delivery

Outside biology entirely, MALDI is also used to read synthetic polymer chains. Because every repeating unit added to a chain adds exactly its own mass, a family of chains of different lengths shows up as a neat row of evenly spaced peaks. That same end-group-counting trick (worked through below) is used to engineer better biosensor surfaces for virus and pathogen tests, tissue-engineering scaffolds designed to dissolve away once real tissue grows in, and drug-delivery patches built to release their cargo on a schedule.

Worked example

A real worked example for the plastic PMMA shows how a single polymer peak can be fully decoded by mass. The peak sits at m/z 2,031. Here is how that number builds up, piece by piece:

  1. One end cap on the polymer chain contributes 115 Da.
  2. The chain has 17 repeat units, each weighing 100 Da, adding 17 times 100 = 1,700 Da.
  3. The other end cap contributes 101 Da.
  4. Five sodium atoms are stuck to the chain, each weighing 23 Da, adding 5 times 23 = 115 Da.
  5. Adding all four pieces together: 115 + 1,700 + 101 + 115 = 2,031.

That total, 2,031, matches the observed peak exactly, which is how a researcher can read a single MALDI peak backward to figure out exactly how long a polymer chain is and what is attached to it.

It is also worth walking through why a top-ranked bacterial or fungal identification is not automatically trustworthy. In one real example, the top hit for a sample was the fungus Fusarium poae, but it scored only 1.180 on the Log(Score) scale, well below the 1.700 cutoff for even a genus-level call. The correct reading of that result is not "this is Fusarium poae"; it is "this sample was not reliably identified," even though the software still displayed a ranked name at the top. A further warning sign in that same result: ten plausible-looking species names from several different genera were all clustered at similarly low scores, from 0.940 to 1.180. A confident identification usually shows one species pulling clearly ahead of the rest, not a crowd of unrelated near-ties. Common reasons for a result like this include a poor-quality or mixed smear, an organism that is simply absent from the reference library, or, for fungi specifically, a sample that needed a longer ethanol and formic-acid extraction protocol rather than the faster direct-transfer method used for routine bacterial identification.

Beyond This Lab, Continued: Other Organisms

Purpose

The previous tab showed that MALDI-TOF is used for far more than this lab's own protein and peptide work, covering bacteria, metabolites and polymers. This tab finishes that general-knowledge tour by looking at a few more organism types: yeast, filamentous fungi, anaerobic bacteria, archaea and mammalian cells. None of this is what this lab's instrument is specifically set up to do, but together these examples show both how powerful MALDI-TOF identification can be and where its limits actually come from.

Key ideas

Yeast and filamentous fungi: the database matters as much as the physics

Clinical yeast identification uses the same whole-cell fingerprint idea covered for bacteria in the previous tab, just matched against a yeast reference library instead. Well-validated studies report species-level accuracy around 99 to 100% once the reference database actually covers the species being tested.

Filamentous fungi (molds) tell a very different story using the exact same physical measurement. Run against a small, generic commercial reference database, that measurement correctly identified only 35.2% of real fungal isolates. Run against a comprehensive, purpose-built library covering far more species, the identification rate for the same kind of measurement rose to 87.4%. Nothing changed about the mass spectrometer or the physics of ionization between those two numbers, only the reference library did. That makes this one of the clearest illustrations anywhere that MALDI-TOF's accuracy ceiling is set by how complete the reference database is, not by the measurement itself.

Anaerobic bacteria: a turnaround-time story

Traditional biochemical identification of anaerobic bacteria (bacteria that grow only without oxygen) could take days to weeks. A four-year real-world clinical audit using MALDI-TOF reached 95.7% species-level identification across more than 4,000 isolates spanning 190 species, a dramatic improvement in turnaround time over the older biochemical methods. Sample preparation still varies by species, though: some need extra incubation time, or an added on-target chemical extraction step, before they give a usable spectrum.

Archaea: a real but early-stage picture

Archaea are chemically different enough from bacteria, with ether-linked lipids and no true peptidoglycan cell wall, that they cannot simply go through a standard bacterial MALDI protocol unchanged. Even so, MALDI-TOF has told apart two archaeal species whose DNA barcode genes are 99.6% identical, a finer resolution than a common DNA test gives. This is a much thinner and newer body of published work than exists for bacteria, yeast or fungi: a real result, but not yet backed by a mature, database-complete method the way bacterial identification is.

Mammalian cells: catching contamination, not identifying species

Mammalian cell lines, the workhorses of cancer research and drug testing, bring a different and less obvious problem: labs regularly lose track of what they are actually growing. A DNA-fingerprinting study of 278 widely used tumor cell lines found that 128 of them, 46.0%, were cross-contaminated by a different cell line or outright misidentified. The single biggest culprit was HeLa, the famous, extremely fast-growing cervical cancer cell line, responsible for 46.9% of all the contamination found, quietly overtaking dozens of supposedly distinct cell lines across different labs.

Intact-cell MALDI-TOF, which reads whole, unlysed cells, uses a much higher mass range for this, roughly m/z 5,000 to 60,000, well above the 2,000 to 20,000 Da window used for microbes. It is increasingly used alongside DNA testing to catch this kind of drift early. The same basic approach also detects Mycoplasma, a cell-wall-free bacterium that commonly and silently contaminates cell cultures: a purpose-built reference library correctly identified every one of 335 validation isolates, though the method needs a fairly dense sample, roughly a million cells per millilitre, to produce a usable spectrum.

Alternative Methods & Future Trends

Purpose

MALDI-TOF is powerful, but it is only one tool on a crowded bench. Other instruments answer questions MALDI-TOF cannot, or answer the same question in a different way with different trade-offs. Knowing what sits next to MALDI-TOF in a real lab, and why someone would reach for it instead, helps you understand why this one instrument was chosen for a given job, and when it would not be.

Key ideas

Other mass spectrometry techniques

ESI-MS and LC-ESI-MS/MS. Electrospray ionization, called ESI, sprays a liquid sample through a charged needle at normal air pressure. The droplets shrink as the solvent evaporates until the molecule itself carries the charge. Unlike MALDI, ESI usually produces ions carrying more than one charge at once, written as [M+2H]2+ or [M+3H]3+. This "multiply charged" habit is the single biggest practical difference from MALDI: it is what lets ESI measure very large, intact molecules, native (non-denatured) protein complexes, and whole antibodies. MALDI is a solid-sample, single-shot method: spot the sample, fire the laser, read a spectrum in seconds, mostly singly charged ions. ESI is almost always paired with liquid chromatography (LC-ESI-MS/MS), a continuous, liquid-phase method that separates a mixture in time before measuring it, usually taking minutes to hours rather than seconds.

GC-MS. A gas chromatograph vaporizes and separates a sample in a long, coiled column, then a beam of high-energy electrons (not a laser) knocks electrons off each molecule as it exits, called electron ionization. This only works for compounds that are volatile and heat-stable, so it cannot touch the large biomolecules MALDI-TOF is built for. Its big advantage is reproducibility: electron ionization fragments molecules so consistently that spectra can be matched against universal libraries such as NIST and Wiley, together with a retention index (a more reproducible version of retention time) as a second check.

DESI-MS and ToF-SIMS. Both are "ambient" imaging methods that, like MALDI, can map molecules across a tissue surface. Desorption electrospray ionization (DESI) sprays charged solvent droplets at a shallow angle onto an untreated surface, with no matrix needed at all, but its spatial resolution has historically trailed MALDI imaging. Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) fires a focused beam of primary ions (bismuth, gallium, or clusters such as C60 or argon) that knock secondary ions straight off the surface, reaching resolution down to the sub-micron or even nanometer scale, far finer than MALDI, but at the cost of breaking molecules into small fragments rather than keeping them intact.

Ion mobility-MS (IMS/TIMS). Here, ions drift through a gas-filled cell under an electric field before they reach the mass analyzer. How fast they drift depends on their collision cross section (CCS, their size and shape, measured in square angstroms), not just their mass. This means two molecules with the exact same mass but a different 3D shape, called isomers, can still be told apart, something a plain MALDI-TOF spectrum can never do on its own. It is an add-on stage bolted onto a TOF analyzer (as in Bruker's timsTOF instruments), not a replacement for MALDI.

Orbitrap and FT-ICR. These trap ions and measure how fast they oscillate, rather than timing their flight. A TOF analyzer typically resolves mass to within tens of parts per million, with a resolving power in the tens of thousands. An Orbitrap or FT-ICR routinely reaches resolving power in the hundreds of thousands, sometimes over a million, with accuracy below one part per million. That precision costs speed (Orbitrap) or a very large, expensive magnet (FT-ICR), while TOF stays fast and comparatively cheap.

ICP-MS. The sample is injected into an argon plasma torch at roughly 6,000 to 10,000 kelvin, hot enough to break every molecule down to its bare elements and ionize nearly all of them. This answers a completely different question from MALDI-TOF: not "what molecule is this" but "which elements, and how much of each, are present." Molecular structure is destroyed on the way in.

Non-mass-spectrometry alternatives

16S rRNA and whole-genome sequencing. Instead of measuring mass, these methods read DNA sequence directly, using a percent-identity threshold (commonly around 98.7 to 99 percent for a confident species call) rather than a MALDI-style score. A head-to-head study comparing three MALDI-TOF systems against 16S rRNA sequencing found strong agreement, 98.6 to 99.7 percent concordance, with most disagreements being minor genus-level calls. One known tricky pair, Streptococcus mitis misread as S. pneumoniae, trips up both methods in their own way. Sequencing takes hours to days versus MALDI's minutes, so most clinical labs run MALDI-TOF first and only sequence when MALDI's spectral library has no match or strain-level detail is needed.

Raman spectroscopy and FTIR. Both read a molecular vibration pattern rather than a mass, and need no matrix at all. Raman shines a laser on the sample and measures the faint light that bounces back at a shifted wavelength (often boosted with metal nanoparticles, called SERS, for weak signals). FTIR instead measures which infrared wavelengths a sample absorbs. Both give a whole-cell chemical fingerprint rather than MALDI's protein-only one, and can sometimes separate very close bacterial strains that look identical to MALDI-TOF. In a 2024 study typing 267 clinical Acinetobacter baumannii isolates into 39 sequence types, SERS combined with a machine-learning classifier reached 99.49 percent accuracy, while MALDI-TOF's own dendrogram could not tell several of those sequence types apart.

NMR spectroscopy. Nuclear magnetic resonance places a sample in a strong magnet and reads how nuclei such as hydrogen-1 or carbon-13 resonate. It needs no ionization step at all, so it avoids mass spectrometry's biggest weakness: uneven ionization efficiency that can hide some molecules from a spectrum entirely. The trade-off is sensitivity: NMR typically needs micromolar-to-millimolar amounts of sample, far more than MS's usual femtomolar-to-attomolar sensitivity.

Classical methods partly replaced by MALDI-TOF. Older identification tools are still used as backups: biochemical panels such as VITEK 2 read color changes from growth tests; immunoassays such as ELISA confirm one specific known target with a label; and Edman degradation chemically reads one amino acid at a time off a protein's end, almost entirely replaced today by mass-spectrometry-based proteomics for anything longer than a short tag.

Worked example

The source material gives this fact: in ESI, a peak at m/z 1,000 carrying a 2+ charge represents a molecule of roughly 2,000 Da, not 1,000 Da. Here is how that works, step by step.

  1. In mass spectrometry, the instrument never measures mass directly. It measures mass-to-charge ratio, m/z: the molecule's mass divided by how many charges it carries.
  2. For a singly charged ion (the usual MALDI case), charge = 1, so m/z and mass are numerically the same. A peak at m/z 1,000 would mean a roughly 1,000 Da molecule.
  3. ESI, though, usually adds more than one charge. If the same molecule instead picks up 2 charges, becoming [M+2H]2+, its m/z becomes roughly half its true mass.
  4. So to get the real mass back, multiply: mass ≈ m/z × charge = 1,000 × 2 = 2,000 Da.
  5. This is why ESI software has to work out the charge state (called charge-state deconvolution) before reporting a mass. Skipping that step, or assuming every peak is singly charged the way MALDI peaks usually are, would make a 2,000 Da molecule look like it weighs only 1,000 Da.

Common misconceptions

  • "If MALDI-TOF can identify something, no other method is needed." MALDI-TOF depends entirely on its spectral library. Database coverage, not physics, is its real limiting factor; sequencing or Raman/FTIR are often brought in exactly when MALDI's library has no confident match.
  • "A peak's m/z value is always the molecule's mass." This is only true for singly charged ions. In ESI, a peak can carry two, three, or more charges, so the true mass is m/z multiplied by the charge, not the m/z number alone.
  • "Higher resolution is always better." Orbitrap and FT-ICR analyzers reach far higher resolving power than TOF, but that precision costs speed and money. For routine, high-throughput identification, TOF's speed usually wins.
  • "These alternative methods compete with MALDI-TOF." Several, like LC-MALDI and the glycopeptide workflow using GlycoQuest and ETD, are built on top of this same MALDI-TOF/TOF instrument, not separate competing machines.

MALDI-TOF is the fast, cheap first pass in a toolkit, and the surrounding methods (sequencing, vibrational spectroscopy, ion mobility, high-resolution MS) get brought in selectively wherever MALDI's own limits, such as database coverage or charge-state ambiguity, actually matter.

Glossary terms introduced

  • Electrospray ionization (ESI): a method that sprays a liquid sample into charged droplets to ionize it, often producing ions with more than one charge.
  • Multiply charged ion: an ion carrying more than one charge, which makes its m/z value lower than its true mass.
  • Charge-state deconvolution: working out how many charges an ion carries so its true mass can be calculated from its m/z.
  • False discovery rate (FDR): an estimate of how many of the reported matches in a database search are likely wrong, usually kept below a set limit such as 1 percent.
  • Electron ionization: an ionization method that uses a beam of high-energy electrons, producing highly reproducible fragmentation, used in GC-MS.
  • Retention index: a standardized, more reproducible version of a GC-MS peak's retention time.
  • Collision cross section (CCS): a measure of an ion's size and shape, used by ion mobility methods to separate molecules of the same mass but different shape.
  • Resolving power: how finely an instrument can tell apart two very close mass values; TOF has lower resolving power than Orbitrap or FT-ICR instruments.
  • Percent identity: in sequencing, how closely a DNA read matches a reference sequence, expressed as a percentage.
  • Surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS): a version of Raman spectroscopy boosted with metal nanoparticles to make a weak signal detectable.

More Worked Examples

Purpose

Reading about mass spectrometry workflows is not the same as practicing them. This tab walks through four realistic research situations on this instrument: deciding how far to loosen a search tolerance, pinning down a phosphorylation site, judging whether a glycopeptide match can be trusted, and reading a protein's ends from a sequencing ladder. Each one shows the questions a researcher actually has to ask of the data, not just the final answer.

Key ideas

Order of operations matters more than loosening a setting. When a database search fails to find a confident match, the instinct might be to immediately make the search more forgiving. The right first step is instead to check whether the data itself has a problem: bad calibration, or contaminating peaks that do not belong to the sample at all.

A dominant signal can hide the evidence you actually need. A phosphorylated peptide's fragmentation spectrum is often dominated by one very strong signal (the loss of the phosphate group itself), which can starve out the weaker signals needed to pin the modification to one exact location.

A percentage alone does not make a match trustworthy. Software can report two different coverage statistics, one about which fragment types appeared and one about how much signal they represent, and a result can score well on one while scoring poorly on the other.

A ladder of peaks reveals structure one step at a time. In sequencing-style spectra, the mass difference between two neighbouring peaks in an ion ladder should equal exactly one amino acid's mass. Where that pattern breaks, something real is usually different about the molecule, not the method.

Worked example

Case: deciding how far to widen a PMF search tolerance.

Research question: a tryptic digest's peptide mass fingerprint (PMF) search comes back with no confident hit at the default, strict 0.1 Da tolerance. Is the protein simply not identifiable, or is something else going on?

Step by step, the way the real workflow handles it:

  1. Check calibration first, not the tolerance. The strict 0.1 Da default only works when calibration is excellent. Check the calibration error on the calibrant spot used for that run. If it is already over 5 ppm, no amount of loosening the tolerance will fix a truly miscalibrated spectrum. Recalibrate before anything else.
  2. Check for autolysis peaks. If calibration looks fine (under 5 ppm), check whether any of the unidentified peaks are actually trypsin autolysis peaks. These show up at m/z 842, 1045, and 2678 in almost every tryptic digest, because they come from the digestion enzyme itself, not the sample protein. A peak list cluttered with them can confuse the database search. Remove them and re-search at the strict 0.1 Da tolerance first.
  3. Only now widen the tolerance, one step at a time. If calibration is clean and no autolysis peaks were the problem, widen the tolerance to 0.5 Da and search again. If still no hit, widen to 1.0 Da. Checking for a hit after each step, rather than jumping straight to the loosest setting, matters because a looser tolerance also raises the odds of a false match.
  4. If nothing works even at 1.0 Da, check the taxonomy or database choice, and confirm the protease really was trypsin.

What a positive result looks like: a confident hit found once calibration is confirmed clean and autolysis peaks are excluded, ideally at the strict 0.1 Da tolerance or with only a modest widening. A hit that only appears at 1.0 Da, with calibration still shaky, is a result to treat with real caution rather than celebrate.

Common misconceptions

  • "If the default tolerance finds nothing, just widen it." Widening the tolerance is the last resort, not the first move. Calibration error and contaminating peaks (like trypsin autolysis peaks) should be ruled out first, because a looser tolerance also makes false matches more likely.
  • "A clean-looking +80 Da mass shift means the phosphorylation site is confirmed." The shifted mass only confirms that the peptide is phosphorylated somewhere. Pinning down which exact residue carries the phosphate needs a clean fragment-ion ladder, which the dominant neutral-loss signal can make harder to see.
  • "A high percentage score always means a trustworthy match." A glycopeptide match can have high Fragmentation Coverage (the right fragment types appear) while having low Intensity Coverage (those fragments represent only a small part of the real signal). Both numbers, and the raw spectrum, need checking together.
  • "Any deviation from a predicted sequence ladder means the experiment failed." A mismatch confined to one end of the ladder, such as a protein's N-terminus or C-terminus, is often real biological information, like a terminal modification or a missing terminal residue, not a mistake.

What to take forward

Before changing a setting to force a result, check whether the data itself, through calibration, contaminating peaks, or a dominant unrelated signal, is the real reason an identification looks uncertain.

Glossary terms introduced

  • Peptide mass fingerprint (PMF): identifying a protein by matching the set of masses from its digested fragments against a database.
  • Calibration error: how far off an instrument's mass readings are from their true values, usually measured in parts per million (ppm).
  • Autolysis peaks: peaks in a spectrum that come from the digestion enzyme breaking down itself, not from the sample.
  • Neutral loss: a fragment of a molecule (such as phosphoric acid) that breaks away during fragmentation and is lost from the measured ion.
  • Fragmentation Coverage: the percentage of a theoretical fragment pattern that actually appears in a spectrum.
  • Intensity Coverage: the percentage of a spectrum's total signal intensity that a proposed match actually accounts for.
  • Ion ladder: a series of fragment peaks, each one amino acid's mass apart, used to read a protein's sequence from one end.

Analyzing with R & Python

Purpose

Every step a researcher does by hand in ProteinScape or flexAnalysis, smoothing a spectrum, removing its wavy noise floor, matching peaks against a reference list, can also be written as code. Learning to read that code, even without being able to write it from scratch yet, means you can see exactly what a "preprocessing step" actually does to the numbers, and reuse the same steps on hundreds of spectra instead of one at a time.

Key ideas

R has a purpose-built toolkit for this exact job. The package MALDIquant (with MALDIquantForeign for reading vendor files such as Bruker's) walks through the same stages described earlier in this guide:

  1. Import raw spectra.
  2. Stabilize variance, then smooth to reduce noise (using a method called Savitzky-Golay smoothing).
  3. Remove the baseline, the wavy noise floor described in the "Reading a Spectrum" tab.
  4. Normalize so different runs are comparable (using total ion current, TIC).
  5. Align spectra to a common m/z scale, since small calibration drift between runs would otherwise look like real differences.
  6. Detect peaks, using a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) threshold.
  7. Bin peaks across all samples so the same biological peak lines up at the same position in every sample, even with tiny mass shifts.
  8. Reshape the result into one row per sample, one column per peak, called a feature matrix, ready for matching or classification.

Python does the same job with general-purpose tools instead of one dedicated package. The scientific stack numpy, scipy, and pandas performs the same steps (smoothing, a rolling-minimum baseline correction, normalizing by total ion current, then peak detection), which is actually a good way to see what each step does, since there is no single function hiding the details.

Matching peaks against a reference list is the coding version of work you already do by hand. Both R and Python can compare an unknown spectrum's peak list against a list of known reference masses within a tolerance window (for example, plus or minus 2 Da), and report what fraction matched, the same logic used throughout this guide's protein identification workflow.

A simple classifier can learn to tell species or classes apart from peak patterns. Using the feature matrix idea (one row per sample, one column per binned peak), a method such as PCA (principal component analysis) can compress many peak-intensity columns into a handful of combined components, which a classifier then uses to assign new samples to a class. This is the same basic idea behind the AI and machine-learning spectrum classifiers described in the "Alternative Methods & Future Trends" tab.

You often do not need a raw spectrum file to get started. ProteinScape's own result tables (the protein table and the peptide table) can be exported directly to Excel from inside the software, by right-clicking the table. That Excel export, read into R with readxl or into Python with pandas.read_excel, is the natural starting point for most people who want to go further than the on-screen results, rather than starting from a raw spectrum file.

Which language to reach for depends on the job. For plain, routine MALDI-TOF spectral processing and classification, R with MALDIquant is the more direct, purpose-built path, since it was written by and for this exact field. For large-scale machine learning, deep learning, or combining spectral data with other data types, Python's broader ecosystem (scikit-learn, PyTorch, pandas) usually pays off. Many labs use both: R for initial spectral quality control, then Python for downstream modeling.

Worked example

Here is the real peak-matching code from this guide, in Python, explained line by line. It checks a list of observed peaks against five real reference masses from the Peptide Calibration Standard II: 757.3992, 1046.5418, 1296.6848, 1347.7354, and 1619.8223 Da.

import numpy as np

reference = np.array([757.3992, 1046.5418, 1296.6848, 1347.7354, 1619.8223])
observed = peaks_df["mz"].values

# absolute difference between every observed peak and every reference mass
diffs = np.abs(observed[:, None] - reference[None, :])
matched = (diffs <= 2).any(axis=0)  # True/False per reference mass
score = matched.sum() / len(reference)
print(f"{matched.sum()} of {len(reference)} reference peaks matched ({score:.0%})")
  1. import numpy as np loads numpy, the package that handles lists of numbers (arrays) efficiently.
  2. reference = np.array([...]) stores the five known calibration masses as a numpy array, so they can all be compared at once.
  3. observed = peaks_df["mz"].values pulls the m/z column out of a table of detected peaks (made earlier by a peak-detection step) as a plain array of numbers.
  4. diffs = np.abs(observed[:, None] - reference[None, :]) is the clever part: by reshaping the two arrays, numpy computes the absolute difference between every single observed peak and every single reference mass, all in one step, producing a grid of differences.
  5. matched = (diffs <= 2).any(axis=0) checks, for each reference mass, whether any observed peak came within 2 Da of it (the matching tolerance). The result is a True or False for each of the five reference masses.
  6. score = matched.sum() / len(reference) counts how many of the five reference masses were matched, and divides by 5 to get a fraction.
  7. The final line prints a sentence such as "5 of 5 reference peaks matched (100%)", in the same way a confidence score is reported for a real identification.

Reading the output: the matching score tells you, in one number, roughly how confident an identification is, the same idea as a Biotyper-style score band, just computed by hand.

Glossary terms introduced

Scenario 10: Mapping an O-GlcNAc site on a transcription factor, and its reciprocal phosphate

Why this fits. O-GlcNAcylation (a single N-acetylglucosamine sugar attached directly to a serine or threonine, written O-GlcNAc) is one of the hardest real post-translational modifications to find. It sits at low stoichiometry, often falls off during ionization or fragmentation before it can be measured, and has no fixed consensus sequence an antibody or a gene search can rely on. A direct mass-shift measurement, exactly the kind of arithmetic used for the amyloid-beta and ubiquitin scenarios above, is one of the few ways to catch it directly and pin it to one residue. O-GlcNAc also tends to sit on, or right next to, the same serines and threonines that kinases phosphorylate, so it shows up again and again on the two protein families this scenario focuses on: kinases and transcription factors, where it can compete with phosphorylation for the same regulatory site, an idea often called the Yin-Yang hypothesis.

Source. Chalkley RJ, Burlingame AL, "Identification of novel sites of O-GlcNAc modification of serum response factor using quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry," Molecular & Cellular Proteomics, 2003, 2(3):182-190, studying serum response factor (SRF), a transcription factor that binds the serum response element (the CArG box) and switches on genes such as c-fos. A limit stated up front: the paper's main sequencing instrument is a Q-TOF, not a MALDI-TOF/TOF. MALDI-TOF (a Bruker Reflex III, DHB matrix) plays a real but smaller role here, screening HPLC fractions of the digest for a telltale GlcNAc signal before the flagged fractions go on to Q-TOF CID MS/MS for sequencing. This is itself a common, honest division of labor in a real proteomics lab, and worth seeing even though MALDI is not the instrument that does the final sequencing in this particular paper.

Aim and research question. The paper's own stated aim is to find where, specifically, O-GlcNAc sits on SRF. Pinpointing the exact modified residues is the necessary first step before anyone can test what that modification does to SRF's activity as a transcription factor; this paper is a site-mapping study, not a functional one, and it is worth being clear about that distinction rather than assuming the sites found here have already been shown to change SRF's behavior.

Workflow.

  1. Purified SRF is reduced, alkylated, and run on SDS-PAGE.
  2. The protein band is digested in-gel, separately with trypsin and with chymotrypsin, giving two different sets of peptides covering overlapping parts of the sequence.
  3. Each digest is separated by HPLC into many fractions.
  4. Every fraction is screened by MALDI-TOF for a sugar-specific signal (the same kind of oxonium-ion signature this guide's glycopeptide tab introduces for N-linked sugars); flagged fractions are the candidates most likely to carry an O-GlcNAc-modified peptide.

What to take forward

Real data rarely arrives as clean as a textbook table: it has missed cleavages, gaps in a fragment ladder, a matrix the authors forgot to name, or a score that needs a second test to confirm. Working through these ten scenarios is practice for exactly that: using the same reasoning this guide's other sixteen tabs teach, on numbers you did not generate yourself, whether that data comes from digested peptides, whole intact protein ions, or a single modified residue buried in a much larger protein.

Glossary terms introduced

  • Expect value: the statistic a MASCOT search reports alongside its score, estimating how likely a match this good would occur by chance; a very small Expect value (such as 1.7 x 10^-6) indicates high confidence even when sequence coverage is modest.
  • Residue mass: the mass a monosaccharide or amino acid adds once it is linked into a chain, after the water lost during bond formation; used to work out composition from a mass difference.
  • Pseudo-precursor ion: a fragment ion (such as one c-ion from an ISD ladder) used as if it were its own precursor mass for a database search, rather than searching the intact protein's full mass.
  • Score band: a range of confidence scores (such as the Biotyper's 2.0+, 1.7-1.9, and below-1.7 bands) that the software or the analyst uses to decide how much to trust an identification.
  • Mass balance check: adding a set of fragment masses together, correcting for the water gained or lost during the reaction that produced them, and comparing the result to the original intact mass to confirm the fragments really came from that one molecule.
  • Top-down fragmentation: breaking apart a whole intact protein ion directly inside the mass spectrometer, instead of digesting the protein into peptides first; contrasted with the bottom-up PMF workflow used elsewhere in this guide.
  • Complementary ion pair: two fragment ions (such as a b-ion and a y-ion) produced from the same single backbone cleavage, whose residue counts or masses together account for the whole precursor ion.
  • O-GlcNAc (O-GlcNAcylation): a single N-acetylglucosamine sugar attached directly to a serine or threonine side chain, adding about 203 Da, the same HexNAc mass gap used for N-linked glycans elsewhere in this guide.
  • Yin-Yang hypothesis: the idea that phosphorylation and O-GlcNAcylation often compete for the same serine or threonine hydroxyl groups across a protein, so gaining one modification can mean losing the other at a nearby or the same site.

Autolysis peak (trypsin autolysis peak): a peak coming from the digestion enzyme itself, not the sample protein. The three to recognize on this instrument are m/z 842, 1,045, and 2,678, and they are excluded from the real peptide list before a search. (Errors; Case Study: PMF; Test Your Knowledge)

AutoXecute: the flexControl feature that runs an automated measurement sequence across a whole plate. (Case Study: PMF)

b-ion / y-ion: the two families of fragment ions produced when a peptide backbone breaks, read from the N-terminal or C-terminal end respectively. (Case Study: PMF)

Baseline: the background noise level a spectrum sits on; the software subtracts it out before picking peaks. (Reading a Spectrum)

Biotyper: Bruker's clinical system for identifying bacteria by matching a whole-cell MALDI fingerprint against a reference library. (Other Applications)

Bottom-up proteomics: identifying and studying proteins by first cutting them into peptides, then measuring those peptides. (Proteomics and PTMs)

C18: a common non-polar column material used in reversed-phase liquid chromatography to separate molecules by hydrophobicity. (LC-MALDI)

Calibrant: a substance of known, exact mass spotted alongside a sample so the instrument's mass readings can be corrected against it. (Sample Prep)

Calibration: matching real peaks to known masses so the rest of a spectrum's masses can be trusted. (Reading a Spectrum)

Calibration drift: when a calibrant peak's measured mass no longer matches its known mass closely enough to trust, the most common cause of a bad result. (Errors)

Calibration error: how far off an instrument's mass readings are from their true values, usually measured in parts per million (ppm). (More Worked Examples)

Carbamidomethyl (C): a fixed, global modification applied to every cysteine in a search, accounting for the alkylation step in sample preparation. (Case Study: PMF)

Carbapenemase: a bacterial enzyme that breaks open a carbapenem antibiotic's ring, making the bacterium resistant to that drug. (Proteomics and PTMs)

Cell line: a population of cells, often from one original source, grown and maintained in culture for research. (Other Organisms)

Centroid vs Snap: two peak-picking modes in flexControl; Centroid reports a peak's whole average mass, Snap reports the monoisotopic mass once isotopes are resolved. (Sample Prep; Reading a Spectrum)

Charge state: how many charges an ion carries; a higher charge state divides its position on the m/z axis. (Reading a Spectrum)

Charge-state deconvolution: working out how many charges an ion carries so its true mass can be calculated from its m/z. (Alternative Methods)

Co-crystallization: the process of the matrix and sample crystallizing together evenly as a spot dries. (Mechanism)

Collision-induced decay (CID): a peptide ion being made to break apart by deliberately colliding it with a gas such as argon. (TOF/TOF, LIFT and ISD)

Collision cross section (CCS): a measure of an ion's size and shape, used by ion mobility methods to separate molecules of the same mass but different shape. (Alternative Methods)

Cross-contamination (cell lines): when a cell culture becomes mixed with or overtaken by cells from a different line without the researcher noticing. (Other Organisms)

Cross-ring cleavage: a sugar ring breaking apart internally rather than at the bond connecting it to its neighbour, producing smaller, characteristic mass gaps. (Glycopeptides)

Dalton (Da): the unit of mass used throughout this guide for peptides, proteins, and mass differences; one Dalton is about the mass of one hydrogen atom. (Cheat Sheet)

Desorption/ionization: the moment the laser's energy vaporizes part of the sample-matrix crystal and gives some molecules an electrical charge. (Overview)

Detector gain: the amplification setting of the ion detector; it needs re-adjusting if its voltage drifts too far from its previous setting. (Sample Prep; Errors)

DNA barcode gene: a short, standardized gene region used to tell species apart genetically. (Other Organisms)

Dried droplet method: the common sample prep method of mixing matrix and sample in solution and letting the drop dry on the plate. (Mechanism)

Electron ionization: an ionization method that uses a beam of high-energy electrons, producing highly reproducible fragmentation, used in GC-MS. (Alternative Methods)

Electrospray ionization (ESI): a method that sprays a liquid sample into charged droplets to ionize it, often producing ions with more than one charge. (Alternative Methods)

Eluate: the liquid coming off a chromatography column, carrying the separated molecules with it. (LC-MALDI)

End cap: a chemical group attached to the end of a polymer chain, with its own fixed mass. (Other Applications)

False discovery rate (FDR): an estimate of how many of the reported matches in a database search are likely wrong, usually kept below a set limit such as 1 percent. (Alternative Methods)

Feature matrix: a table with one row per sample and one column per peak, used as the input to matching or classification. (R and Python)

Filamentous fungi: mold-forming fungi that grow as branching thread-like structures, harder to identify by MALDI-TOF than yeast because reference databases cover them less completely. (Other Organisms)

Fragmentation Coverage: the percentage of a theoretical fragment pattern that actually appears in a spectrum. (More Worked Examples)

FT-ICR-MS: a technique that measures mass using a magnetic field instead of a flight tube, for extremely high precision. (Mechanism)

Glycopeptide: a peptide with a sugar chain (glycan) attached at a specific site. (Glycopeptides)

Half-moon effect: a visible crystallization failure pattern caused by too much organic solvent drying the spot too fast. (Errors)

Hard ionization: an older, rougher ionization method (such as electron beam bombardment) that destroys fragile molecules. (Mechanism)

Hexose, HexNAc, sialic acid (NeuAc): three types of sugar units commonly found in glycan chains, each with its own fixed mass contribution. (Glycopeptides)

In-source decay (ISD): a technique that reads a ladder of fragments directly off an intact protein, spontaneously breaking its backbone in the ion source before the ions start flying, without digesting it first. (Overview; TOF/TOF, LIFT and ISD)

Intact-cell MALDI: a MALDI-TOF measurement taken on whole, unlysed cells rather than a digested or extracted sample. (Other Organisms)

Intensity: the quantity plotted on a spectrum's y-axis; how many ions of a given mass were detected. (Reading a Spectrum)

Intensity Coverage: the percentage of a spectrum's total signal intensity that a proposed match actually accounts for. (More Worked Examples)

Ion ladder: a series of fragment peaks, each one amino acid's mass apart, used to read a protein's sequence from one end. (More Worked Examples)

Ion-mobility TOF (IMS-TOF-MS): a coupled technique that separates ions by shape as well as mass. (Mechanism)

Ion suppression: when high-abundance molecules dominate ionization and hide low-abundance ones in the same spectrum, unless the mixture is separated first. (LC-MALDI; Test Your Knowledge)

Isobaric: having the same total mass, so labeled peptides from different samples overlap in the plain MS spectrum until fragmented. (LC-MALDI)

Isotope peak: one of a small cluster of peaks, about 1 Da apart, caused by naturally occurring heavier isotopes of the same molecule. (Reading a Spectrum)

iTRAQ: an isobaric labeling method that lets up to 8 samples be compared in a single MS run. (LC-MALDI)

Kinetic energy: the energy an object has because it is moving; it depends on both mass and speed. (TOF/TOF, LIFT and ISD)

LIFT: on this instrument, the naming convention and the physical cell used to reaccelerate fragment ions so a whole MS/MS spectrum can be read at one fixed reflector voltage. (Sample Prep; Case Study: PMF; TOF/TOF, LIFT and ISD)

Linear mode / Reflector (reflectron) mode: two ways of timing ion flight. Linear mode sends ions straight to a detector at the tube's end and is needed for very large, intact molecules. Reflector mode bounces ions off an electric mirror back to a detector near the source, giving sharper, more accurate peaks, but only works well for smaller molecules. (Overview; Mechanism; Glycopeptides)

Liquid chromatography (LC): a technique that separates molecules in a mixture by pumping them through a column that holds some of them back more than others. (LC-MALDI)

Log(Score): the confidence number a Biotyper-style result reports for each candidate match; higher means more reliable. (Other Applications)

MALDI-TOF: Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization, Time-Of-Flight mass spectrometry: a technique that uses a laser and a light-absorbing matrix to turn a biological sample into flying ions, then measures their masses by timing their flight. (Overview)

MALDIquant: an R package purpose-built for importing, preprocessing, and analyzing MALDI-TOF spectra. (R and Python)

MASCOT ions score: the confidence score MASCOT assigns to a search hit, compared against a significance threshold to decide if an identification is trustworthy. (Errors)

Mass accuracy: how close the instrument's reported mass is to the true mass, often given in parts per million (ppm). (Overview)

Mass discrimination: the instrument's built-in tendency to detect light ions more easily than heavy ones, even when equal amounts are present. (Mechanism)

Matrix: a small, light-absorbing chemical mixed with the sample that protects it from the laser's energy and helps it become charged. (Overview)

Metabolomics: the study of small molecules (metabolites) in a sample, such as sugars, lipids and amino acids, using mass spectrometry. (Other Applications)

MH+: the mass of a molecule plus one added proton, the form in which it is usually measured and reported in positive-ion MALDI. (Glycopeptides)

Monoisotopic mass: the mass of the lightest isotope peak in a cluster. (Reading a Spectrum)

Multiply charged ion: an ion carrying more than one charge, which makes its m/z value lower than its true mass. (Alternative Methods)

Mycoplasma: a cell-wall-free bacterium that commonly and silently contaminates laboratory cell cultures. (Other Organisms)

m/z (mass-to-charge ratio): the value plotted on a spectrum's x-axis; equals mass divided by the number of charges on the ion, so it equals mass itself only when the ion carries a single charge. (Overview; Reading a Spectrum)

Near-neighbour calibration: calibrating a sample spectrum using the calibrant spot or calibration fit physically closest to it, for the best accuracy. (Sample Prep; Case Study: PMF)

Neutral loss: a small, uncharged chemical group (such as phosphoric acid) breaking off a peptide during fragmentation, seen as a drop in mass rather than a separate charged peak. (Proteomics and PTMs; More Worked Examples)

N-terminus / C-terminus: the start and end, respectively, of a protein's amino acid chain. (TOF/TOF, LIFT and ISD)

Oxidation (M): an optional, variable modification allowed on methionine in a search, since oxidation during handling is not guaranteed to have happened. (Case Study: PMF)

Oxonium ion: a small, positively charged, sugar-only fragment (for example at 204 Da or 366 Da) that appears at a fixed mass regardless of which protein it came from, used to flag a glycopeptide spectrum. (Glycopeptides; Cheat Sheet)

PCIS (PreCursor Ion Selector) window: a gate, with a mass window set around it, that lets through only one chosen parent ion and its matching fragments before LIFT MS/MS measurement, blocking everything else. (Case Study: PMF; TOF/TOF, LIFT and ISD)

Percent identity: in sequencing-based identification, how closely a DNA read matches a reference sequence, expressed as a percentage. (Alternative Methods)

Peptide mass fingerprint / PMF (peptide mass fingerprinting): identifying a protein by matching the masses of its digested peptides, or the set of masses from its digested fragments, against a database. (Overview; Case Study: PMF; Proteomics and PTMs; More Worked Examples)

PMF search tolerance: how far, in Da, a measured peptide mass is allowed to differ from a database value during a peptide mass fingerprint search. (Errors)

Post-translational modification (PTM): a small chemical change added to a protein after it has been built. (Proteomics and PTMs)

ppm (parts per million): the unit used to express a small measurement error, such as calibration error or a search tolerance, as a fraction of the measured mass. (Errors; Cheat Sheet)

Principal component analysis (PCA): a method that compresses many correlated measurements (such as peak intensities) into a smaller number of combined components. (R and Python)

Random Walk: a flexControl setting that moves the laser automatically around a spot during measurement. (Errors)

Reference library / database: the stored collection of known spectral fingerprints that a new MALDI-TOF measurement is matched against. (Other Organisms)

Repeat unit: the small chemical unit that repeats many times to build up a polymer chain, each one adding the same fixed mass. (Other Applications)

Resolution: how finely the instrument can tell apart two very close masses; higher numbers mean sharper, better-separated peaks. (Overview; Reading a Spectrum)

Resolving power: another name for resolution, used when comparing TOF against other instrument types such as Orbitrap or FT-ICR. (Alternative Methods)

Retention index: a standardized, more reproducible version of a GC-MS peak's retention time. (Alternative Methods)

Retention time: how long a molecule takes to travel through and come off an LC column. (LC-MALDI)

Reversed-phase: an LC setup where more hydrophobic molecules stick to the column longer and come off later. (LC-MALDI)

Ribosomal proteins: highly abundant proteins that are part of a cell's protein-building machinery, useful for whole-cell fingerprinting. (Other Applications)

Signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio or threshold: the minimum ratio of peak height to background noise the software requires before it will count something as a real peak. (Sample Prep; LC-MALDI; R and Python)

SILAC / ICPL: quantitation labeling methods whose signal is visible already at the plain MS level, limited to comparing 4 samples at once. (LC-MALDI)

Surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS): a version of Raman spectroscopy boosted with metal nanoparticles to make a weak signal detectable. (Alternative Methods)

TA30: the standard solvent used for the HCCA matrix, 30 percent acetonitrile and 70 percent 0.1 percent TFA in water. (Sample Prep)

Target plate: the metal plate with many spots where matrix-sample mixtures are deposited and dried before measurement. (Sample Prep)

Time-of-flight (TOF): the part of the instrument where ions travel through a vacuum tube and get sorted by how fast they move, which depends on their mass and charge. (Overview)

TOF1: the first flight tube in a TOF/TOF instrument, where ions first separate out by mass before MS/MS. (TOF/TOF, LIFT and ISD)

Tolerance window: the allowed mass difference (for example, plus or minus 2 Da) when deciding whether an observed peak matches a reference mass. (R and Python)

Total ion current (TIC): the sum of all signal intensity in a spectrum, used as a reference to normalize spectra so different runs are comparable. (R and Python)

Trypsin: an enzyme that reliably cuts a protein chain right after every lysine or arginine, used to produce measurable peptides. (Proteomics and PTMs)

WARP-LC: the automated software that manages an LC-MALDI run, tracking peptides across spots and scheduling MS/MS. (LC-MALDI)

ZipTip: a small pipette-tip device (commonly C18) used to desalt or clean up a sample before trying MALDI again. (Sample Prep; Errors)
